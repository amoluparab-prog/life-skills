# Toast writer: a short spoken tribute, from true material

A tribute works when the room recognises the person in it. That comes from true, specific details only the speaker has, so this skill gathers those first and writes second. The speech belongs to the speaker: their stories, their plain words.

## Rules that hold throughout

- **Invent nothing.** No story, quote, date, achievement or feeling that the speaker did not give you. Where something is missing, leave a blank in square brackets that says what goes there, such as `[how you two met, one line]`. Never fill a blank yourself, not even "as an example". Offering to make something up is also out.
- **Do not look up the people named**, online or anywhere. Work from the speaker's words. Web lookups are for customs only (section 3).
- **Nothing at anyone's expense.** No jokes about a body, age, weight or looks, about past partners, or about anything the person would not want said in front of this audience. Toast, do not roast. If the speaker asks for a roast, tease only about things the speaker says the person laughs about themselves; that is also the only way one of the topics above gets in.
- **Say so when a story is risky** (section 2). The speaker decides whether a story stays, but they hear your concern first, and a story that stays is told the safer way.
- **Inside jokes are explained in one line or cut.** A line only four people understand loses the rest of the room.
- **No song lyrics, hymns or poems written out, and no famous quotations supplied by you.** Such texts are often under copyright, you may get the words wrong, and a reading means more when the speaker chose it; tell them so in a line. If the speaker wants a reading, leave a slot, `[your reading here, about 20 seconds: you choose it and read it yourself]`, and count its time. If they paste lyrics or a poem, or ask you to pick one, still leave the slot: do not copy the text and do not choose the piece. Saying what a half-remembered piece is, so they can find it, is fine. A saying of the person's own, as the speaker reports it, is fine.
- **Customs are the speaker's to describe.** Follow religious, cultural and family customs as they tell them. Never guess customs from a name or a country.
- **Alcohol is optional.** "Please raise your glasses" works with any drink. If the speaker says there is no drinking, or you do not know, close with a wish or "please join me in wishing..." in place of a glass.
- **Plain words, the speaker's voice.** Short sentences that are easy to say aloud. No grand phrases, no dictionary definitions, no "for those who don't know me" apologies. Write in the language it will be spoken in.

## 1. Gather the material

Skip what the request already answers. Ask the rest in at most three numbered questions per message, stories first. One question may bundle quick facts that belong together ("who will be in the room, and is there a microphone?"). Needed before writing:

- **Must have:** the occasion and who it is for; how the speaker knows them; at least two true, specific stories or details.
- **Ask once, in the same message as a must-have question or in the second round; if no must-have is missing, ask nothing and assume and state:** time allowed, tone (warm, light, formal), who will be in the room (ages, family, colleagues, how many), the setting (microphone, seated meal, service), where it is and any customs, and anything that must not be mentioned.

If stories are slow to come, prompt memory; do not suggest content. For example: "What is a small habit of theirs everyone in the room would recognise?", "Tell me about a time they turned up for you.", "What did you think when you first met them, and what changed?" For a wedding, also ask for one true thing about the partner and about the two of them together.

Ask at most two rounds of questions (flags from section 2 can ride along with the second round and do not count as questions), then write a first draft with blanks for whatever is still missing. People find it easier to correct a draft than to answer a fourth round. If the material stays thin, write a shorter speech: a true 60 seconds beats a padded three minutes.

**If the user is away, says "just write it", or cannot reply:** ask nothing. Write everything in section 6 from what you have, list your assumptions at the top, mark blanks, cut any flagged story by default, say so, say which cut would be easiest to put back and how, and end with an invitation to send corrections, not a question.

## 2. Screen what you were given

For each story ask: would the person honoured, and the oldest and youngest listener, be glad to hear this said here? It may also be filmed and shared. Always flag: bodies and looks, past partners, money, health, drinking or drug stories, arrests, secrets and anything told in confidence, and for a eulogy the manner of death and family disputes.

For each flagged story say in one line why it is risky, then offer a safer telling (keep the warmth, drop the detail) or a cut; until they answer, leave it out of the draft. Suggest the speaker tries a doubtful story on someone close to the person first. If the speaker still wants it in, write the safer telling and note the choice. An inside joke you cannot explain, because nobody told you the story behind it, becomes an optional blank for the speaker to explain or drop. List every cut in the flags so nothing vanishes silently.

## 3. Customs and the order of things

Use the place and tradition as the speaker described them. If you have a web tool and the speaker named a tradition, or a place and the kind of event, look up (a handful of searches at most) who usually speaks and in what order, who is toasted, and anything usually avoided; read the page, name it and the day you read it, and add "families differ; check with the hosts". With no web tool, or when pages will not open, say so and put these under "To check" as questions for the host, the family or the person leading the service. Never state a custom from memory as fact.

## 4. Size it

Words = minutes x 130 (a comfortable speaking pace in English; a eulogy x 110, because it is spoken more slowly). For other languages keep the minutes and tell the speaker to time it aloud. If no time was given, use and state: toast 2 minutes; wedding, birthday, anniversary, farewell or retirement speech 3 minutes; eulogy 4 minutes, or whatever the family or the person leading the service allows. Treat the time as a ceiling, not a quota: the written words plus the time for blanks and any reading slot (count about 15 words per one-line blank, and a slot's seconds as words) should use most of it and never go over; convert at the same pace you sized with. Not padding comes first: if the true material runs out sooner, stop sooner, say so, and say that one more true story would fill the time. Show the count and the sum. If asked for more than about 5 minutes (about 10 for a eulogy by the only speaker), say that shorter usually lands better, then do as asked.

## 5. Write the speech

1. **Opening.** Who the speaker is and how they know the person, in one line, unless the whole room knows. Then straight to the person.
2. **One or two stories** that each show a quality. Tell them so a stranger could follow: who, where, what happened, what it shows. One good story beats three thin ones.
3. **What the person means** to the speaker, said plainly, from the speaker's own words. If they gave none, sum up what the stories show and leave a blank for their own line; do not write feelings for them or for anyone else. At a wedding, turn to the partner and the couple here. Keep the speaker in the background.
4. **The close.** A wish, then one short line the room can repeat: "To [name]." It follows "please raise your glasses" where there are drinks and "please join me in wishing..." where there are none or you do not know. Do not end on a story.

**Eulogy.** Gentle pace and shorter sentences. No humour unless the speaker gave a fond, funny memory; then tell it as they told it, laughing with the person, never at them. Keep the person recognisable, not perfect. Close with words to the person or what they leave behind, not a glass. Remind the speaker to follow the family's wishes and to ask the person leading the service where the eulogy fits and how long it may be.

## 6. Give these, in this order

1. **Assumptions and flags** (only if any): what you assumed, stories you softened or cut and why. Anything the speaker must do before the day, such as agreeing the text with whoever leads the service, goes here at the top, not only under To check.
2. **The speech**, with word count and estimated time.
3. **Speaking copy.** The same words, not a rewrite, one thought per line, marked: `/` a short pause, `//` a long pause (count two), `(look up)` where to look at the person or the room, `(wait)` after a line that may get a laugh, **bold** for the one word to lean on in a line. Use marks sparingly, about one per line. Blanks carry over as they are.
4. **20-second version**, about 40 words: who you are, the one truest line, the toast or closing wish. For when time is cut or nerves win.
5. **Delivery notes**, fitted to this speaker and setting: print the speaking copy on numbered cards in large type, not a phone (if it must be a phone: flight mode, screen set to stay on). Know the first and last lines by heart and read the rest. Read it aloud three times and time it; nerves speed people up, so slow down. Wait for the room to settle before starting. No drink for courage beforehand. If emotion comes: stop, breathe, look at the card, sip water, go on; no apology is needed. For a eulogy: bring water and a tissue, and give a copy to someone who can finish reading it if needed.
6. **To check:** blanks to fill, anything you worked out from what they said (such as a number of years), customs to confirm, names to pronounce.

Then ask once what does not sound like them, and revise, showing only the parts that changed and the new count unless they ask for everything again. (If the user is away, the invitation from section 1 replaces this question.) The same rules hold on every later turn: a new story is screened, a request to "add a funny bit" gets a memory prompt, not an invented joke.

## When not to use this

A business presentation, pitch, keynote, lecture or panel talk; a political or campaign speech; a message that will only be written (a card, a gift note, a social post); wedding vows or an officiant's ceremony script; planning the event itself; the steps of a religious ceremony. Say it is outside this skill and answer normally or hand off. In a mixed request, write the speech and hand off the rest.

## Example

(All names invented.)

User: "Write a farewell speech for my colleague Tovin Marlesh, here are some stories. He's leaving after 9 years. I'm Ressa, I've sat next to him for 6. Friday, about 30 of us from three floors in the office kitchen, managers included, 2 minutes. He labels everything, even the stapler. When I started he stayed till 8 pm three nights to help me pass my licence exam. I passed, and he never brought it up again. Also he once fell asleep in a client meeting after a big night out, ha."

Reply, in outline. The must-haves are all there, so no questions. Flags: the client-meeting story is left out of this draft (drinking, and managers are in the room), with a safer telling offered if Ressa wants it back; the labels stay because the whole office sees them. Assumed: warm and light, no microphone. Speech, 240 words plus one blank counted as 15, about 2 minutes: one line on who the speaker is; the labelled stapler; the three late nights and what they showed; a blank for what that meant to her; a wish; "please join me in thanking Tovin. To Tovin." Speaking copy, first lines:

```
I'm Ressa, and for six years / I have sat one desk from Tovin.
If you have ever borrowed our stapler, you know it is **his** stapler. (wait)
```

20-second version: "I'm Ressa, and I have sat next to Tovin for six years. When I started, he stayed late three nights to get me through my exam, and never brought it up again. That is who we are losing. Tovin, thank you. To Tovin." Delivery notes fitted to a kitchen with no microphone: stand where everyone can see, speak to the back wall. To check: whether drinks are being served; if so, the close can become "please raise your glasses: to Tovin".
