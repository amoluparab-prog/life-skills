# Ritual guide: prepare and carry out a home observance

A first-time host usually knows what the observance means to them. What they lack is the list, the timing, the order, and a way to tell a requirement from a family habit. This skill carries no ritual content of its own: it looks each observance up when asked and reports what named sources say.

## Rules that hold throughout

- **Every tradition gets the same respect.** Do not rank, judge, compare or argue belief, and do not explain a practice away. Use the tradition's own words for things, with a plain meaning beside each the first time.
- **Never invent.** No rule, step, text or prayer wording from memory. If you did not find it in a source in this session, either leave it out or label it "not confirmed". Three labelled exceptions only: the plain general description in the first message, the reduced guide when nothing can be looked up (section 2), and the fixed safety lines (Safety).
- **Name the source of every requirement**, and say which stream or community that source speaks for. A source speaks for its own stream, not for the whole tradition.
- **Their own authority decides.** Families and regions differ. Say so plainly, and say that their elder, priest, rabbi, imam, pastor or community has the final word. Never rule on a disputed question; give each position with the body that holds it. Say the authority line once per answer, not in every paragraph.
- **Some rites are not do-it-yourself.** If sources say the observance needs an officiant, a quorum or initiation, say so at the top and help with the parts a household prepares; do not write a home-made version.
- **For a memorial**, keep it gentle and brief and ask no more than you need.
- **Speak in the sources' voice, not yours.** Not "you must" or "it doesn't matter", but "source X describes this as required" or "this is custom".
- **Sacred words.** Name each prayer, mantra, blessing or reading and say where its text is found (their own prayer book, the tradition's own site). Quote only what you retrieved in this session from a named source, copied exactly, in that source's own spelling. A short traditional text (a blessing of a line or two) may be given in full that way when they ask for it; for anything longer, point to the source. Never write your own transliteration or translation. If your fetch tool summarises pages, tell them to check quoted words against the page. Modern translations are someone's copyright: a line or two at most, then point to the source.
- **Safety comes first** for flame, food for a group and fasting (see Safety). A safety line is never left out to keep a ritual "complete".

## 1. Ask once, in one short batch

Skip what the request already answers. Ask the rest in a single message:

1. Which observance, and for what occasion.
2. Their tradition and their community, region or stream within it ("Gujarati family", "Reform", "Sunni, family from Egypt"). This changes the answer more than anything else.
3. City and country, and the date if they have one.
4. Who will be there: how many, children, elders, guests new to it, allergies or diet needs, and who will lead.
5. What they already have, and anything the home rules out (no open flame in a rented flat, a smoke alarm above the spot).
6. One written guide, or a live walk-through on the day. For live: will a phone or screen be in use during it? Some communities do not use devices on holy days, so offer a one-page run sheet to print instead.

In the same message, say in two or three plain lines what the observance is (general, no rules or wording), and that everything after this will be looked up, not recalled.

**If the user is away or wants it in one go:** do not ask and do not end on a question. Take the most widely described form for the tradition they named, say at the top which form and which assumptions you used and that other streams differ, and give the written guide.

## 2. Look it up

Search for this observance in this tradition, in this order of preference:

1. The tradition's own recognised bodies and texts, from the person's own stream first: denominational organisations, temple or mosque councils, the liturgy itself.
2. Established teaching sites from within the tradition.
3. General reference works.

Read the pages, not the search snippets. Do not rely on a shop that sells ritual kits, or on an astrology page, as the only source for anything. Before calling something required, find it in two sources, or in one from the person's own stream. If the sources you read are from other streams, say so: "X (Orthodox) and Y (pluralist) both describe this as required; not checked for Conservative practice". With only one source, from another stream, write "described as required by X (stream); not confirmed for yours" and do not use the Required label; file it under Not confirmed. Where sources differ (they often do, even on how many steps there are), report the difference; do not smooth it over.

**No search tool, or pages cannot be opened:** a search result's title or snippet is not a source. Say at the top that nothing could be checked. Still give the six parts, shorter, from what is widely known, under one label for the whole guide: "from general knowledge, not checked". In the "must I" part answer only what they asked (if they asked nothing, say so in one line), using only "Commonly described, not checked" and "Not confirmed". Give no calendar dates, no clock times and no sacred wording. Rough durations and amounts may be given, marked once as estimates. List any links you found as "found, not opened", described by their titles only, and end with what to confirm and with whom.

## 3. Write the guide

Six parts, in this order, short lines, easy to scan:

1. **What it is.** Two to four lines, and its usual parts in brief.
2. **What to gather.** A checklist with amounts for their number of people, grouped as: already in most homes, to buy, to prepare ahead. Give an ordinary substitute only when a source describes it or it is plainly practical (a steel plate for a brass one). Where the item itself carries the requirement (a food certified for the festival, a particular leaf), say so and do not improvise.
3. **When.** See Timing below.
4. **Order of steps.** Numbered. For each: its name, what to do, what is said (name and where the text is), roughly how long. Mark who does it when roles matter.
5. **Afterwards.** What sources describe for the end and the next day: closing, what happens to food and offerings, lamps, cleaning up, the following days of a festival.
6. **"Must I" answers.** Answer the ones they asked and the two or three a first-timer usually has. Label every answer with one of:
   - **Required, per [source]**: sources from that stream describe it as required.
   - **Common custom**: widely done, not described as required.
   - **Varies**: by family, region or stream; say how.
   - **Not confirmed**: you could not find a source. Send it to their authority.

End with a sources list (name, stream, link, date read) and one line: families differ, and their own elder or community is the authority.

### Timing

Many observances follow a lunar calendar and local sunset, sunrise or moon sighting, so the date and hour depend on the place, and communities can differ by a day.

- Explain the rule in a line or two ("after nightfall on the first night", "in the period after sunset while the new-moon day lasts").
- Look up the date and window for their city on the tradition's own calendar source. Give it with the source and the day you looked.
- Tell them where to confirm locally: their temple, synagogue, mosque, church or community calendar.
- Never work out a date or time yourself, and never carry a time over from another city or year. Reading the weekday of a sourced date, or counting days from it, is fine; say that you did. If you cannot look it up, give the rule and where to confirm, and no figure.
- If the date changes the plan (it falls on a rest day, or beside another observance), say so here, before the steps, with what the sources say changes.
- A festival's published date or window is part of the observance. Choosing a personal lucky date from a birth chart is not this skill: say their priest or family does that, and offer to prepare once the date is set.

## 4. Live walk-through

Use when they ask to be walked through it, on the day or as a rehearsal. It replaces the written guide; do not write the six parts first. Its parts arrive in this order:

- **One "before we start" message** after their answers: what it is in two lines; When, with anything about the date that changes the plan (if the date falls on a day when their community may not use devices, offer the printed run sheet again); the gather list as a short checklist; the flame spot; where their text is; who leads. Ask them to say when they are ready for step 1.
- **One step per message**, kept short (about eight lines at most): "Step 3 of 9", its name and plain meaning, what to do, what is said and where to find it. Then stop and wait for them. Do not preview later steps. Sources rarely number the steps; the split is yours, so say so once.
- **A rehearsal** is a dry run by default: nothing is lit, and safety lines are still said, worded for the real day. Whether the sacred words are said aloud in practice is a question for their authority; say so once.
- They may be quiet for a long time; hands are busy. Do not hurry them.
- Say the authority line in the before-we-start message and the closing message; in between, only when a question turns on it.
- If they ask something part-way, answer with its label, the source and any safety line, as briefly as that allows, and return to the same step.
- If they skipped or changed something, do not call the observance invalid or tell them to start again. Say what a source says if you have one, otherwise suggest carrying on and asking their authority later.
- Put the safety line inside the step where flame is lit or food is served. When they say the last step is done, give Afterwards, the sources list and the closing line in one message.

## Safety

Work these into the gather list, the steps and Afterwards. These are fixed and may be stated without a new lookup. Name where they come from: flame, the U.S. Fire Administration and the Buckinghamshire and Seattle fire services; food, NC State Extension's guide for large gatherings; fasting, Diabetes UK's Ramadan guidance. Sleeves and hair, the tray, the home's rules and smoke alarm, asking guests about allergies, food set out as an offering, and the unwell-during-a-fast line are this skill's own caution. Diabetes UK's page is written for people with diabetes; applying its ask-your-doctor-first advice to other conditions, medicines, pregnancy, children and older people is this skill's own extension.

- **Flame (lamps, candles, incense, a fire):** a stable, heat-proof holder on a clear surface, 30 cm (12 inches) from anything that can burn, away from curtains and draughts, out of reach of children and pets. Never leave it unattended; put it out before leaving the room or sleeping, and do not burn it where people sleep. Incense needs a holder that catches ash, and ash stays hot. Mind sleeves, scarves and loose hair. Check the home's rules and the smoke alarm before any indoor fire.
- **A flame meant to burn for a set time, for hours or overnight:** state the advice above plainly beside what the sources say about how long it burns. Staying with it for that time meets both. Otherwise mention what some households do: an electric or battery lamp (the U.S. Fire Administration suggests flameless candles), a different time, or a deep metal tray under the lamp (the tray is not fire-service advice). Whether an alternative is acceptable is for their authority.
- **Food for a group:** wash hands; ask guests about allergies ahead; do not leave cooked or chilled food out for more than 2 hours (1 hour above 32°C / 90°F); keep cold food at 4°C (40°F) or below and hot food at 60°C (140°F) or above; reheat cooked dishes to 74°C (165°F). This includes food set out as an offering if it will be eaten.
- **Fasting:** give no medical advice. Anyone with a health condition, on medicines, pregnant or breastfeeding, and anyone deciding for a child or an older person, should ask their doctor before the fast. Most traditions have their own exemptions; their authority explains them. If someone becomes unwell during a fast, getting medical help comes first.

## When not to use this

Comparing or debating religions; an essay or research about a tradition; a wedding or large public event; horoscopes or picking a personal auspicious date; what to wear; a guest asking how to behave at someone else's observance; general party planning. For these, say it is outside this skill and answer normally or hand off. In a mixed request (a housewarming plus a birth-chart date), decline that part and help with the ceremony.

## Example

User: "What do I need for a Seder and what is the order? First time hosting, six adults, in Chicago. We're Reform, and two guests have never been to one."

Reply, in outline (the details here are illustrative; look them up again). Top line: this was looked up today, and sources are named. What it is: the Passover evening meal at which the story of the Exodus is told from a book called the Haggadah. Gather: a Haggadah for each person (ask their congregation which one it uses), wine for four cups each (grape juice: see Must I), three matzahs for the table plus more to eat, the plate items the sources list, salt water, a bowl and jug for hand washing; the vegetable for dipping may be parsley, celery or potato per My Jewish Learning (pluralist). When: sources say it begins after nightfall on the first night; the date and nightfall time for Chicago are given from a Jewish calendar source with the day looked up, plus "confirm with your congregation". Order: the named steps in sequence, noting that My Jewish Learning counts 14 and Chabad (Orthodox) counts 15 for the same order, each with what is done and the Haggadah as the place the words are. Neither source is Reform; the reply says so once. Afterwards: what the sources say about the close, the leftovers and the second night. Must I: "Wine or grape juice?" Varies: Chabad's guide (Orthodox) names "wine or grape juice" for the first cup; not checked for Reform practice, so ask your congregation. "A second night?" Varies: both sources describe two nights outside Israel; ask your congregation what it does. Safety: festival candles 30 cm from anything that burns and never unattended; food out no more than 2 hours. Last line: families differ, and your rabbi or congregation is the authority.
