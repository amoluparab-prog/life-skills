# learn-plan: a fixed-time plan to a goal you can test

Learning plans often fail in the same ways: the goal is vague ("learn Spanish"), the plan is a list of things to read or watch, nothing is tested until the end, and one missed week ends it. This skill gives one plan that avoids those. Work through the steps in order.

## Rules that hold throughout

1. **Honest about time.** Never promise fluency, mastery or any result. Show the hours as a sum and say what that many hours can and cannot do. Practice explains only part of how well people do, and people differ, so write "a realistic target", not "you will".
2. **No invented resources.** Name a course, book, app, channel or teacher only if you opened its page in this session ("checked today": it exists, is still offered, price seen) or the person named it ("as you told me"). A well-known one you are sure exists may be named from memory, marked "unchecked: confirm it still exists and what it costs"; at most two such names in a whole plan. A name seen only in a search listing counts as one of the two and is marked the same way; a listing is not a check, and a page opened with no price shown is "checked today, price not seen". If unsure, describe the kind of resource and give the search words; never make up a title.
3. **No steering.** Free options first when they will do the job. Plain links only, no affiliate or tracking tags, no "sign up here"; for a phone app give its name to search in their own app store, since store links are tied to a country. Sign up for, buy and book nothing.
4. **Physical risk.** Where a mistake can injure (swimming, climbing, lifting, martial arts, riding or driving anything, power tools, mains electricity or gas, diving, strenuous dance or gymnastics), say which parts need a qualified instructor in person and build the plan around those lessons; give no technique for the risky parts yourself. The practice tasks for those parts are then "what the instructor set", and the plan covers the timetable, recall of what was shown, and the checks. On days without a lesson there is no new thing: that part goes to warm-up and recall of what was shown. If the goal cannot be practised safely alone, say so first and plan a safer goal, or the same goal done with a qualified person; more time alone is never the fix. Everyday risks such as kitchen knives and hot oil need no instructor: add a short safety line and let technique come from the stage's resource. For any skill learned through the body, such as an instrument or touch typing (not ordinary desk work): short sessions, a warm-up (its minutes come out of the new-thing part), and stop on pain; pain that returns is a question for a health professional, not for the plan.
5. **Children.** If the learner is under 18, write the plan to the parent or guardian, who chooses and supervises any app, account or teacher. Under about 13: home sessions of 10 to 20 minutes even if more was offered (use the length you plan, not the length offered, in the sum; a teacher's lesson keeps its own length, and a lesson day has no home session), playful, rest days, and weekly checks presented as showing what they can do, with the marks kept for the adult. For a teenager the usual session and checks stand, with rest days and no pressure. If a child writes alone, give a light version (goal, session shape, first week; no accounts, no paid resources) and ask them to show it to a parent.
6. **Licences.** Some activities need a formal qualification, permit or inspection (often driving, flying, diving, electrical or gas work, medicine, law), and the rules differ by place. Look up what applies where they live, or mark it "to confirm" with the official body named; do not state a legal requirement from memory. If one applies, say so plainly, say the plan only prepares for or adds to the official route, and offer no way round it. If none is found, say what you found and where, and rule 4 still holds.
7. **Their needs, their words.** If the person mentions something that affects how they learn (slow reading, poor sight or hearing, pain, tiredness, trouble sitting still), change the format, session length and the check to fit, using their own words. Do not name, guess or suggest a condition.
8. **Their learning, done by them.** Explaining, drilling and marking practice are fine. Writing graded coursework or answering a live exam is not; offer practice on the same material.
9. **No country is the default.** Ask or infer where they are; use their currency, and look for resources in a language they read. Create no reminders or calendar entries unless asked.

## Step 1. Get the essentials

Use what the request already says. Then ask once, at most four short questions of one or two facts each, only for what is missing and would change the plan; assume the rest and say so. The essentials:

- what they want to be able to do, and why (trip, job task, performance, pleasure);
- by when, and how many minutes on how many days a week;
- where they are starting from (never tried, tried and stopped, rusty);
- how they like to learn (video, book, teacher, with others, by doing), and anything that makes learning harder for them;
- budget, country, and what they already have (instrument, software, someone to practise with).

If they are away or say "just give me the plan", ask nothing: state your assumptions in two or three lines at the end. No date given: assume four weeks ("a month" is four weeks too). No time given: assume 20 minutes, five days a week; "most days" is five and "every day" is six, with a rest day. Say these are assumptions. If the start day is unknown, use day or session numbers ("day 14", "after session 10") in place of dates.

## Step 2. The goal, and what the time buys

- **The sum:** days x minutes = total hours ("30 days x 40 min = 20 hours"). Count only practice days. Add an instructor's lessons at their own length if known; otherwise say the sum leaves them out.
- **The goal, restated so it can be tested:** who does what, under what conditions, without what help. "Hold a five-minute conversation ordering food and asking directions, with a patient speaker, no phone." If they gave a vague goal, offer one restated goal and go on with it.
- **What it will not reach:** one or two plain lines ("not following fast native speech, not reading a newspaper").
- If the goal clearly does not fit the hours, say so first and offer two ways out: a smaller goal for the same date, or the same goal with a longer, stated time. Then plan the smaller goal.

## Step 3. What matters and what to leave out

List three to five sub-skills that the goal depends on most, each tied to the goal in a few words. Then a short "leave out for now" list with the reason. Leaving things out is what makes a short plan work; say that once.

## Step 4. Week by week

Give each week a focus, the sub-skills it covers, and what "done" looks like. For plans up to six weeks show every week; for longer plans show the first four in detail and the rest as phases. Do not script every day: give the session shape once, then for each week its practice task. Build sessions like this, and tell the person why in a line or two:

- **Start with recall (about a quarter of the session; in the very first session, give this time to the new thing and its practice).** Without notes, write, say or play what was learned in earlier sessions, then check against the source. Recalling from memory holds better than rereading, and the check matters. Recall means producing the answer, not picking it from a list.
- **One new thing, small (about a quarter).** A short explanation or demonstration, from the stage's main resource.
- **Practise it with feedback (about half).** A defined task just beyond what they can do, repeated, with a way to see errors: an answer key, a recording of themselves played back, a working spreadsheet or program, a teacher or partner.
- **End with a one-line note:** what to recall next time.

Across the weeks:

- **Short and spread out.** Several short sessions usually beat one long one for the same total time. Bring each item back the next day, a few days later, and about a week later.
- **Mix once the basics are in.** Learn a new thing on its own first; from the second week, mix older and newer items in the recall and practice parts. Mixing feels slower and often pays off later; for a complex task or a young child, keep more repetition of one thing before mixing.
- **Use the real thing early.** From week one, a small version of the goal itself (a two-line exchange, one song section, one real spreadsheet task).
- **Last days:** no new material; rehearse the goal under its real conditions.
- **Reading and watching are input, not practice.** Keep them to about a quarter of any session, even for someone who likes to learn by watching or listening: their preference picks the format (audio that makes them answer aloud, video they copy), not the share. Highlighting and rereading feel useful and rate poorly.
- One rest day a week at least.

## Step 5. Resources, one main and one backup per stage

A stage is a week or a group of weeks that use the same material. For each stage give a main resource and a backup, of a different kind where possible (if the main is a video course, the backup is a book or a person). With a search tool, spend about eight lookups (any rule 6 lookup comes first and counts): open the page of each resource you name and note what it is, who makes it, the price or "free", the date seen, and any sign that it is out of date or closed. A page that is refused or fails is not retried; the resource becomes "unchecked". Look for local options too (a library, a community class, a conversation group) when the person's place is known. For each resource say which part to use; a whole course is rarely the plan.

## Step 6. A weekly check with a pass mark

One check at the end of each week, ten minutes or less, done cold: no notes, no help, first attempt counts. State the task and a number as the pass mark ("order a meal from this menu aloud, recorded, in under two minutes, with no more than three stalls"). For open tasks such as a conversation or a song, count something: stalls, restarts, items right, or time. Where they cannot judge themselves (pronunciation, technique, safety), say who or what can: a recording compared with a model, a teacher, a speaker of the language. Tell them to write the result down. For a child, and for any part under rule 4, the check happens in the lesson or with the instructor or supervising adult there, and may run longer; "no help" never means unsupervised. For a rule 4 part the instructor sets or confirms the task and the pass mark: propose a plain, observable target for the person to take to them, and set no technique target of your own.

- **Pass:** go on.
- **Just under:** go on, and put the weak part in next week's recall.
- **Well under:** repeat the week's weakest half before moving on, and make room by dropping the least important sub-skill still to come, not by adding hours.

## Step 7. Missed sessions

Count planned sessions missed, not calendar days.

- **One session:** carry on with the next one. Do not double up.
- **Two sessions up to just under a week of the plan (two to four in a five-day plan):** come back with a recall-only session, then pick up where they stopped; drop the least important item from the remaining weeks.
- **A whole week of the plan or more:** redo the last weekly check and restart from the week it shows. Then move the date, or, if the date is fixed by an event, shrink the goal; say which and restate the goal.

## Step 8. Review point

Set a date about halfway (for plans of two weeks or less, after the first third). They answer three questions: are the weekly checks passing; is the daily time really happening; is the goal still the one they want. Say what changes for each "no": slow down and cut scope, shorten sessions or move them in the day, or restate the goal. Offer to redo the plan from their check results.

## The plan, in order

The goal and the hours sum, in three or four lines; what it will not reach; sub-skills and leave-outs; the session shape; a week table (week, focus, practice task, check and pass mark); resources by stage with their labels; missed sessions; review date; assumptions, and "unchecked". Anything from rules 4 to 6 that applies goes first when it changes the goal, otherwise after the review date. Every part appears; keep each one short (a full plan is usually 700 to 1,000 words, a little more when rules 4 to 6 apply) and cut words, not parts. If they asked for something short, or said long text is hard for them, give the goal, the week table and the checks, plus anything from rules 4 to 6 that applies, and offer the rest in short pieces.

## If a tool is missing

No web search: say in one line near the top that nothing was looked up. Give the whole plan anyway; steps 2 to 4 and 6 to 8 need no lookups. For step 5, describe each resource by kind with the search words to find it, add names only as rule 2 allows, and list what to confirm (still offered, price, level).

## Worked example

A session with no search tool. The person writes:

> "I'm going to Italy in 30 days. I can do 20 minutes a day. Never learned any Italian. I'd like to get by in restaurants and shops. I don't want to pay for anything. Just send me the plan."

They said "just send me the plan", so ask nothing. Outline of the reply, shortened here; a real reply also says why sessions start with recall, what "done" looks like each week, what to do when a check is under the mark, and what changes for each "no" at the review:

> Nothing was looked up for this plan; the resources are described by kind for you to find.
>
> **Goal:** by day 30, order a meal and buy something in a shop, aloud, with a patient speaker, without your phone. **Time:** 26 practice days x 20 min = about 8.7 hours. That is enough for set phrases and simple swaps said clearly. It is not enough to follow fast replies or hold an open conversation; a realistic target, not a promise.
>
> **Matters most:** sounds and stress so you are understood; about 20 survival phrases; numbers and prices; about 60 food and shop words; "sorry, slower please" and "I'd like". **Leave out for now:** grammar tables, past and future tenses, directions, reading and writing beyond menus.
>
> **Every session:** 5 min say earlier phrases from memory, then check against the audio; 5 min one new set with audio; 10 min the week's practice task.
>
> | Week | Focus | Practice task (the 10 min) | Check (cold, no notes) | Pass |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | 1 | Sounds, greetings, numbers 1 to 100 | say prices aloud from a list, record, compare | Say 10 prices read from a list, and 8 greetings and polite phrases from memory | 15 of 18 |
> | 2 | Restaurant | order from a real menu found online, aloud, recorded | Order a starter, main and drink aloud, recorded, under 2 min | No more than 3 stalls |
> | 3 | Shops, mixed with weeks 1 and 2 | buy three things from a made-up shop list, asking sizes and prices | 6 mixed prompts drawn from cards | 5 of 6 |
> | 4 | Rehearsal, nothing new in the last four days | full role-plays aloud, both sides | The goal itself, two scenes, with a partner if you have one, otherwise recorded | 2 scenes, each under 2 min |
>
> **Resources.** Weeks 1 to 2, main: a free beginner audio course with a transcript (search "free Italian audio course beginners travel"); backup: a phrasebook from your library. Weeks 3 to 4, main: your own cards, made from weeks 1 and 2; backup: a free conversation group or exchange partner, online or near you. All unchecked: confirm each is still free and aimed at beginners. You can also practise the role-plays with me.
>
> **Missed sessions:** one, carry on; two to five (this plan has six sessions a week), one recall-only session, then continue and drop the shop words beyond prices; a whole week, redo the last check, restart from the week it shows and shrink the goal to restaurants only, since the trip date is fixed. Never double up.
> **Review on day 15:** checks passing? 20 minutes really happening? Goal still right?
> **Assumed:** four rest days; you have a phone to record yourself. Tell me your country and I will suggest where to look locally.
