# Weekend plan: days off that neither drift nor fill up

Days off go wrong in two ways: nothing is decided and the time leaks away, or every hour is filled and nobody rests. This skill plans one main thing a day (the "anchor"), protects rest, and names only places and events it has checked or clearly marked as unchecked.

## Rules that do not bend

1. **Stated place only.** Use the town or area the person tells you. Never guess it, never take it from memory of earlier chats, and never use a location tool unless they ask you to. If you do not have it, ask.
2. **Nothing invented.** Every place or event you name carries one of three marks: **confirmed** (you read the venue's own page or an official listing in this session and it shows that date and time; give the page name and the date you read it), **unconfirmed** (seen only on a general listing site or in a search result, or it is a permanent place you know of but did not check), or **to check** (not looked up). Never give a dated event, a price or opening hours from memory. Last year's date for a yearly event is unconfirmed. A permanent place whose own page you read is "confirmed (regular hours)": say it is the standing timetable, not a listing for that date. Mark fact by fact when you read some and not others (hours confirmed, price to check). Confirmed means listed on that page, not that tickets remain; if a day off is, or may be, a public holiday (they call it a long weekend, or you are not sure), put "holiday hours" for the venue and the transport under to check.
3. **Forecasts are dated.** Give the forecast with its source and the day and time you read it, say that it can change, and say when to look again (the evening before). No forecast from memory.
4. **Do not overfill.** At most one anchor a day and one other planned thing (a second outing or visit counts; fixed commitments, meals, the chore block and the reset do not). Rest is a planned part of the weekend, not what is left over.
5. **Their limits stand.** Budget, faith observances, food rules, nap and bedtimes, and caring duties are fixed points. Plan around them without comment. Show the cost as a sum against the budget.
6. **No alcohol-centred ideas** (bars, tastings, brewery tours) unless they ask.
7. **Outdoor ideas carry a safety note** (see below). Name the official body for that place if you read its page; if you did not, give the general lines below and say they are general (the lines for going in the water follow the International Life Saving Federation's open-water guidelines; the others are common outdoor practice), and put the official source under "to check".
8. **Book nothing, buy nothing, send nothing.** Calendar entries and reminders only if they ask: show the exact list first and wait for a yes.
9. **No country is the default.** Ask which days are off (a weekend is not Saturday and Sunday everywhere, and shift workers have their own). Use their currency, units and time format. If they are away, state the days as an assumption, and if no currency is given use bare numbers.
10. **Keep what they share out of searches.** Search by town and date, not by their names, address or family details. Save nothing unless asked.

## How to run it

**Person present:** one short message asking only for what is missing, then the plan. If they answer only part, plan with what you have and mark the rest as assumptions. **Person away, says "just give me ideas", or gave enough:** ask nothing; open with the short answer and the one assumption that matters most. If the place is missing and they are away, still give every part of the plan, describe anchors as kinds of thing with no names, and make the place the first item under "To check". **No web tool, or pages refused:** do not retry; follow "When you cannot look things up". If only some pages fail, mark just those items to check and keep what you did read.

### Step 1: gather (only what is missing)

- Where they are (town or area) and how far they are willing to travel.
- Which days are off, and any fixed commitments in them: matches, lessons, worship, visits, work shifts, naps and bedtimes.
- Who is involved: ages, energy, anything about walking, stairs, seating, toilets, prams or wheelchairs.
- How the week was, and the mood they want: rest, adventure, people, or getting things done. One main mood; a second is fine.
- Budget for the whole weekend and the currency. "Not much" is an answer: plan free things first.
- Transport: on foot, bicycle, public transport, car.
- Food rules and whether anyone wants to cook.
- Chores that cannot wait, and anything they already hope to do.

### Step 2: look up (keep it to a handful of pages)

If lookups are few: the forecast first, then the own page of the idea you would pick, then access for their group, then the rest.

- **Forecast and warnings** for each day off, from the national weather service of their country. If you could only read another official service's page, name it and put the local one under "to check". Note sunset time if anything outdoors could run into the late afternoon or evening.
- **What is on and open** on those dates. Prefer, in this order: the venue's or organiser's own page; the town, city or regional council's events page or the official tourism office; the park, forest, trail or beach authority; the public library; the club's or league's fixture page. General listing sites are for finding things, not confirming them.
- For each idea: day and time, cost for their whole group, how to get there and roughly how long door to door by their transport (your estimate is marked as an estimate), whether booking is needed and by when, and access notes that matter for their group.

### Step 3: shape the days

- **Fixed commitments go in first.** A fixed commitment that fills a good part of a day (a family lunch, a match, worship) is that day's centre: add no anchor to it unless they ask.
- **One anchor a day**, about two to four hours including travel. Give it a rough slot ("Saturday morning"), not a clock time, unless a ticket, a tide or a kick-off fixes one. Studies of leisure find that an exact time can make free time feel like a task, while having a plan makes it more likely to happen; the authors suggest a rough slot as the middle way.
- **Choose anchors by mood and by the week they had.** Time off tends to restore people when it lets them switch off from work, relax, do something absorbing or new, and decide for themselves. After a heavy week lean to the first two. Always offer two or three ideas and let them pick: the choosing is part of the rest.
- **Different kinds of thing on different days** (one out, one in; one active, one slow; one with people, one without). Variety helps across days; do not pack several different things into one short slot.
- **Rest block.** At least one half-day with nothing planned, named in the plan as rest. For families, rest includes the adults.
- **One chore block at most**, two hours or less by the clock, as early as the fixed commitments allow, with an end time. It may run while another adult is at a fixed commitment. If the chores they call must-do will not fit, put the part that cannot wait in the block (clean clothes for the week, not the whole pile), list the rest under "not this weekend", and say you did.
- A day with no commitments still gets one small anchor, even a walk or a film at home, so the day has a centre, unless they ask for a day of nothing.

### Step 4: the rest of the plan

- **At-home alternative:** one idea for bad weather or low energy that needs nothing bought or only what is on the buy list. Say what would trigger the switch ("if the warning is still in place on Friday evening").
- **Meals kept easy:** one meal made ahead or from what is in the house, one simple shared cook if someone wants to, and eating out only if the budget holds it. Keep to their food rules. No recipes unless asked.
- **Book or buy today:** each item with its deadline and where; say plainly if nothing needs booking.
- **Last-evening reset:** 20 to 30 minutes on the last evening off: look at the week's calendar, put out clothes and bags, make sure the first breakfast and lunch are sorted, set a bedtime. Home things only, no work. If a week-planning skill is installed, say in one line that it can plan the week itself.

### Outdoor safety notes

Give the two or three lines that fit the idea.

- **Weather:** check the official forecast and any warning the evening before and on the morning; if a warning covers that place, time and kind of activity, follow it and change to the alternative.
- **Daylight:** for anywhere without lighting or easy exits (trails, hills, open water, countryside), give the sunset time (looked up) and plan to be finished an hour before it. Lit streets, parks and waterfronts in town need no more than the sunset time, and only if the outing could run late. If you could not look the time up, put it under to check.
- **Water (sea, river, lake, pool):** go with others; choose places with lifeguards; follow signs and flags; check water and weather conditions first; children get close, constant attention in or near water; life jackets for children and weak swimmers on boats and open water; no alcohol before going in.
- **Beside water, not swimming:** children within reach; stay off wet rocks, and off piers and breakwaters in rough weather.
- **Trails and hills:** check the land manager's page for closures; stay on marked routes suited to the least able person; carry water and layers; tell someone the route and return time. For anything beyond an easy marked walk, point to the local mountain or outdoor safety body and do not plan the route yourself.
- Heat, cold, sun, air quality, insects or wildlife where the official forecast or the local authority flags them.

### When you cannot look things up

Say so in the first lines. Then:

- Still give the full shape, the at-home alternative, meals, the buy list and the reset: none of them needs the web.
- Give anchors as **kinds of thing** that fit the season, group and budget ("a morning market", "a flat riverside walk"), each with what to search for and which official page would confirm it. You may name a permanent public place you are sure exists (a main park, the coast), marked unconfirmed. Name no dated event, price or opening hours.
- Forecast: "to check", with the name of the official weather service if you know it, and a plan that works either way.
- Cost: for anything whose price you could not read (here, or when only some pages failed), give spending caps you set inside their budget, labelled as caps and not prices, with an amount held back for charges they may find. Keep the caps under the budget where you can.
- End with a short "To check" list in the order they should do it.

## The plan (in this order, kept short)

1. **In short:** two lines, with the main assumption.
2. **Shape:** a small table, one row per day off: morning, afternoon, evening. Rest and the chore block appear in it.
3. **Anchor ideas:** two or three, each with: what and where; day and rough time; cost for the group; travel; booking; status with source and date read; safety note if outdoors. Say which you would pick and why in one line.
4. **Forecast:** per day, source, read when, look again when.
5. **At-home alternative.**
6. **Meals.**
7. **Book or buy today.**
8. **Last-evening reset.**
9. **Cost:** the sum, against their budget.
10. **Assumptions** and **To check**, if any.

Close by offering one thing only: to swap an anchor. If they ask for calendar entries, a rough slot needs a clock time: propose one, say it can be moved, show the list and wait for a yes. With no calendar tool, give the entries as text to copy; never say an entry was added unless the tool reported it.

## Handing on

If an anchor grows into a trip with a night away, a full timed route round a city, or a gathering they host, say in one line that a trip, day-route or hosting skill fits that part, and plan the rest of the weekend around it.

If they say something that goes beyond a tiring week (not coping, weeks without sleep), say kindly that a weekend plan cannot fix that and that someone they trust or a health professional can, then offer a lighter weekend if they want one.

## Worked example (every name invented)

> Ideas for the long weekend with two kids and not much money? We're in Harbourton, off Saturday to Monday, kids are 4 and 9, no car, about 40 in our money for everything. Rough week. The little one naps 1 to 3. Football practice Saturday 10 to 11.

Enough is given, so no questions. In short: one outing on Sunday morning and one on Monday morning, afternoons at home around the nap, total 31 of 40; I have assumed 90 minutes of chores on Saturday. Shape: Saturday, practice, then chores 11:30 to 1, rest afternoon; Sunday, anchor in the morning, nap, quiet evening; Monday, anchor in the morning, rest, reset at 7. Anchor ideas: (1) Harbourton Council's "Lantern Trail" in Mill Park, Sunday from 10, free, about 15 minutes by bus (my estimate), no booking: confirmed, council events page, read Thursday; outdoors, so look at the forecast again on Saturday evening (general advice). (2) Tidewater Museum family morning, Monday, 12 for the family as listed: unconfirmed, seen on a listing site only, so check the museum's own page before going. (3) A flat walk on the harbour path with a picnic: permanent place, unconfirmed; mornings; the 4-year-old within arm's reach near the water (general advice; the harbour authority's page is to check). Forecast: Saturday and Monday dry, rain likely Sunday afternoon (national weather service, read Thursday 6 pm; it can change, so look again Saturday evening). At home if it pours: a blanket-den film afternoon. Meals: a pot of soup made Saturday for two days, picnic from the house, one bakery treat (cap 4). Book or buy today: nothing to book; bus day tickets for Sunday and Monday (15 in all, bus operator's fares page, read Thursday). Cost: museum 12 (unconfirmed) + buses 15 + treat cap 4 = 31 of 40, with 9 held back. To check: the museum's own page; holiday hours for the museum and the buses if Monday is a public holiday. Nothing goes in a calendar, because they did not ask.
