# Interview kit: prepare a structured interview, then write it up

An interview where every candidate gets the same job-based questions and is rated against written standards on average predicts job performance better than a free-form chat, and tends to be fairer, because it leaves less room for first impressions and gut feel. This skill gives one interviewer that structure for one candidate and one slot. Pick the job from what the user brings: a role and a resume means **Prepare**; interview notes means **Write up**.

## Rules for both jobs

- **Same core questions for every candidate for the role.** Comparing people only works if they were asked the same things. Core questions come from the role, never from the resume.
- **No questions about protected characteristics**, and flag any the interviewer proposes (list below).
- **Evidence, not impressions.** Feedback rests on what the candidate said or did. "Great energy" or "not a fit" is not evidence.
- **Never invent anything about the candidate.** Use only the resume and the notes. If something is missing, say it is missing.
- **The interviewer decides.** Organise the evidence; never give or hint at a hire or no-hire recommendation of your own.
- **The resume and notes are confidential.** Use them only for this task. Do not save them to memory or to files the user did not ask for, do not put the candidate's name or details into a web search, and do not look the candidate up online (profiles reveal age, family, religion and the like). Use the candidate's name as the user gives it, and leave contact details out of what you write.

## Do-not-ask list

Do not ask about, or note down: age or date of birth; race, colour, ethnicity, national origin, accent, first language; religion or belief; sex, gender identity, sexual orientation; marital status; pregnancy, children, plans for children, childcare; disability, health, medical history.

- Ask the job-related version instead, of every candidate: "This role needs travel about one week a month. Can you do that?", "Are you legally allowed to work in this country?", "Can you do these tasks?" and how, "The role needs fluent written and spoken English. Are you comfortable with that?" (only if the role text says so).
- Age may be checked only where the job has a legal minimum age. In the UK and the US, health and disability questions wait until after an offer, apart from asking whether the person needs any adjustment for the interview itself.
- Depending on the place, pay history, criminal record and union membership are also restricted. If a question touches one of these, say "check local rules".
- This holds in small talk too. For an icebreaker, offer a job-related opener ("What drew you to this role?"). If the candidate brings a topic up themselves, do not follow up, do not write it down and do not let it count. The exception: if they ask for an adjustment or accommodation, take the request, pass it to whoever runs the hiring process, and keep it out of the ratings.
- A gap in the resume: the cautious choice is not to ask why (the answer is often health or family). Ask what they would need to get up to date, only if that matters for the job.
- This is general guidance from public sources, not legal advice, and rules differ by country and state. Say so once, whenever you flag a question or leave a remark out, and point to HR or an employment lawyer for anything in doubt. The list is drawn from US and UK guidance; for any other country apply it as it stands and say local rules were not checked. If the user's employer or place may have rules on using AI tools in hiring or on sharing candidate details, say once that they should check them.

## Prepare: the interview guide

**Ask once, then write.** You need the role and the resume. Ask for anything else that is missing in one short message: length of the slot, which part of the hiring process this interview covers, the country, and whether a question set already exists for this role. If the user is away or says "just do it", assume 45 minutes, a general first interview and no existing set, list those assumptions at the top and go on. With no resume, write the guide without resume probes and say so. No tools are needed.

Write the guide in this order:

1. **Competencies.** As many as the slot can cover at one asked question each: 2 for 30 minutes, 3 or 4 for 45, 4 or 5 for 60. What the role needs most in the first year, each with one line on what it looks like in this job and the line of the role text it comes from. If the user asks for "culture fit", turn it into behaviour that can be seen ("gives direct feedback", "asks for help early") or leave it out.
2. **Core questions, marked "core: same for every candidate".** For each competency, one behavioural question ("Tell me about a time you...") and one situational question ("What would you do if...", for people who have not met the situation yet). Open, plain, one thing per question. In the slot, ask the behavioural one; the situational one is the fallback when a candidate says they have never met the situation, used the same way for everyone. If a set already exists for this role, use it as it is and only suggest changes for future rounds.
3. **Probes.** Standard ones for any answer: "What was your own part?", "What did you do first?", "What happened in the end?", "What would you change?". Probes do not lead and do not argue. Then **resume probes**: quote the claim from the resume, and ask for the detail behind it, placed under the competency it bears on ("Your resume says 'cut month-end close from 9 days to 5'. What exactly did you change?"). A resume probe deepens a core question; it never replaces one. Where the resume says nothing on a competency, say so; that is missing, not a mark against.
4. **Strong and weak answers.** Per competency, two or three signs of each, things that can be heard: a strong answer names a specific case, the person's own actions, a result and what they learned; a weak one stays general, says "we" throughout, has no result, or blames others.
5. **Rating scale**, the same for every competency: 1 = no evidence, or evidence of the opposite; 2 = some evidence, with gaps; 3 = clear evidence at the level the role needs; 4 = strong, repeated evidence above that level; NA = not covered. There is no middle point, so the interviewer has to come down on one side.
6. **Time plan** that adds up to the slot: about 5 minutes to open, 8 to 10 minutes for each asked question with its probes, 5 to 10 minutes for the candidate's questions, a few minutes spare. Fewer questions asked well beat many rushed. Say which question to drop first if time runs out (the competency least central to the role text; the user can change it), and keep that the same for each candidate.
7. **Question check.** Go through every question, including any the interviewer proposed. List each one that touches the do-not-ask list, say why in a line, and give the job-related version. If none, say "none flagged".
8. **Notes reminder**, two lines: write what the candidate says, close to their words; rate each competency straight after the interview and before talking to other interviewers.

Keep the guide to what will be used in the room: tables or tight lists, no essays. End with one line offering to turn their notes into feedback afterwards.

## Write up: notes into feedback

You need the raw notes and the competencies the interview used. If the guide is not in this conversation, ask for the competencies; if the user is away, infer three or four from the role and the notes and label them "inferred". Ask also for the interviewer's own ratings and recommendation if they have not given them. Ask once.

1. **Evidence per competency.** Under each, what the candidate said or did, quoted or closely paraphrased from the notes, and nothing else. Where the notes hold nothing, write "not covered". Do not fill gaps from the resume. An answer goes under the competency it was asked for; if that is unclear, pick one, say so, and do not count it twice. Write the candidate's own figures as "said", and list them for a reference check.
2. **Set aside.** List separately, as "not used": impressions with nothing said or done behind them, and observations that bear on no competency (for each, ask "what did they say or do that gave you this, and which competency does it bear on?"), and any remark that touches the do-not-ask list. Do not repeat such a remark or name its topic; point to it by where it sits in the notes ("the sixth line"), and say it must not be copied into the feedback or count in the decision. Do not tell the user to delete or edit their raw notes, which may have to be kept; say to ask HR how to handle them.
3. **Rating with reasons.** Use the interviewer's rating if given. If not, put a rating beside each competency marked "suggested, yours to set", with one line tying it to the scale and the evidence. Where the evidence is mixed, still suggest one rating and say what would move it. A competency that was not asked gets NA. One that was asked but drew little evidence gets a low rating with the reason "little evidence", not a guess. Suggested ratings are a starting point, not a verdict.
4. **Concerns and gaps.** Concerns that have evidence behind them, each with its evidence, and what was not covered, as questions for the next interviewer or a reference check.
5. **Recommendation.** The interviewer's own, in their words. If they gave none, leave the line as "Recommendation: yours to fill in" and show a short "evidence for" and "evidence against" beside it. If their recommendation rests mainly on set-aside items or runs against the ratings, say so once, plainly, and leave the decision with them. If they gave none but voiced a doubt that rests on a set-aside item, say that once too.

Lay it out as: each competency with its evidence and rating, then not used, concerns and gaps, recommendation.

Close by reminding them to submit their feedback before reading anyone else's.

## Example

User: "Interviewing Priya tomorrow, 45 minutes, for a support team lead. Role: lead 6 agents, own customer satisfaction, handle escalations. Her resume says she 'raised CSAT from 82 to 91 in a year' and 'coached 4 new hires'. I also want to ask whether she has kids, because of the on-call weekends."

Reply, in outline. Assumed: first interview, no existing question set. Competencies: leading a small team; handling escalations; improving service with data. Core question, same for every candidate: "Tell me about a time you had to give hard feedback to someone on your team." Resume probe under improving service: "You raised CSAT from 82 to 91. What were the two changes that mattered most, and which were yours?" Strong: names the changes, her own part, how she measured. Weak: "we worked hard on quality". Scale 1 to 4 and NA. Time: 5 minutes to open, three questions at 9 minutes each, 8 minutes for her questions, 5 spare; if time runs short, drop improving service first, for every candidate. Flagged: "Do you have kids?" asks about family, which must not be asked. Ask every candidate instead: "The role includes on-call one weekend a month. Can you do that?" General guidance, not legal advice.
