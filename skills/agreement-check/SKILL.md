# Agreement check

This skill reads the agreement from the person's side and gives them something they can act on before they sign. The reader is not a lawyer: use everyday words, and explain any legal term the first time it appears.

## Rules that hold throughout

- **Only what is in the document.** Every finding rests on a quote from the text, with its clause number or heading. If something is not there, say "not stated". Never describe "typical" terms as if they were in this document; an invented term is worse than no review.
- **Never rule on enforceability.** Do not say a clause is or is not legal, valid, binding or enforceable where the person lives, even if asked directly. Law differs by country, state and city, and changes. Say what the clause asks of them, that rules on this kind of clause differ by place, and who can tell them (a local lawyer, or an official tenant, worker or consumer body). Name a specific body only if you are sure it exists and deals with people in their position (a business buyer is not always covered by a consumer body); otherwise describe the kind. If the agreement picks the law or courts of a different place from where they live, report that as a finding and leave it there.
- **If they ask directly** ("is this even legal?"), still do not rule. Answer in two or three lines straight after the verdict: you cannot tell them, why, who can, and that getting the clause changed before signing avoids the question.
- **Not legal advice, said once.** One clear sentence near the top. Do not repeat it in every section.
- **Confidential.** Do not save the document or its details to memory or to files the person did not ask for, do not paste its text into web searches, and do not repeat names, addresses, pay or account numbers beyond what the review needs.
- **Nothing invented about the person either.** In draft messages, put anything you do not know (their rate, their reasons, names) in [square brackets] for them to fill in.
- **Never sign, send, accept or click agree**, even if tools allow it. Suggested messages are drafts for the person to send themselves.

## 1. Set up

Check four things before reviewing, using what the request already says:

1. **The document.** If no text was given, ask for it (pasted, attached, or a link to public terms) and stop; do not review from the type alone or list "typical" risks. Keep that reply to a few lines; if they gave a deadline, say you will lead with the most urgent points once you have the text. If an attachment cannot be read or a link cannot be opened, say so and ask for pasted text. If it looks cut off, or it refers to other documents that were not provided ("House Rules", "our policies as updated", a schedule), review what you have and list what is absent under Missing.
2. **Their side.** If it is plain from the request ("my lease", "they want me to sign"), state it and go on. Ask only if it truly cannot be told, because the same clause is good for one side and bad for the other.
3. **The type**, from the document itself. If it is two types at once (subscription terms that are also terms of service), use both lists below. Reply in the language the person wrote in; quote the document in its own language with a short translation, and say the translation is yours and the original wording is what counts.
4. **Where they are and any deadline**, only if mentioned. Do not ask for these up front; place is used only to point them to the right local body.

If the person is away, cannot reply, or asked for everything in one go, ask nothing: state your assumptions in the first lines and give the full review.

If the agreement is already signed, still explain it, but say that changes now need the other side's consent, and, for a purchase or subscription, that some places give a short period to cancel after signing; point them to who can tell them, without saying whether it applies. Word the verdict as what they have agreed to. Keep the change message (or Your choices, where step 7 says so), worded as a request to record the changes in a written amendment both sides sign. Drop "Before you sign" and move its useful lines (promises in writing, missing documents, keep a copy) into the message or Missing. If a dispute is already under way, explain what the text says and stop there; that needs a lawyer or an advice service, not this skill.

## 2. Read all of it

Read from the first line to the last, including definitions, schedules and anything in small print. Watch for these, whatever the type:

- Money beyond the headline price: fees, deposits, late charges, interest, price rises, costs passed on.
- Automatic renewal, and how long before the end date notice must be given.
- Getting out: who may end it, on what notice, at what cost. Is it the same for both sides?
- The other side changing the price or the terms later without the person's consent.
- Limits on the other side's responsibility while the person's is unlimited; promises to cover the other side's losses or legal costs.
- Limits on what the person can do afterwards: working for competitors, approaching clients, speaking about the deal.
- Handing over rights: ownership of work or ideas, use of their data or content.
- Disputes: private arbitration instead of court, no group claims, a distant court, loser pays costs.
- Repayment if they leave early: training, sign-on bonus, relocation, equipment.
- Blanks, "to be agreed", and anything promised in conversation that is not written down.

## 3. Write the review

Use this order. A long review does not get read: for an agreement of a page or two aim for about 1,000 words in all (the table and the message count), and let longer documents grow slowly, to about 1,800 words for ten pages or more. Keep every section. If the draft runs more than about a third over, cut in this order: Low findings to one line each, Questions to three, Missing to four, the two duty lists to four items each. Never cut a High finding, the table, or the change requests for High findings. The opening lines must stand on their own, because a hurried reader stops there.

1. **Opening lines.** What the document is, which side you read it from, any assumptions, and the not-legal-advice sentence. Then a verdict of two or three lines: the biggest issues and whether it looks ready to sign as it stands. If they are away, gave a deadline within about two days, or said they are short of time, add "If you only do three things" with the three actions that matter most.
2. **Who must do what.** Two short lists, "You must" and "They must", each item with its clause, six items at most per list. If the document puts no duty on one side, say so; that is itself worth knowing.
3. **Key terms table.** Rows for money (every amount and when due), dates (start, end, deadlines), renewal, and exit (notice, fees). Columns: term, what it says, clause. Write "not stated" where the document is silent. Add two worked figures when the numbers allow, and no more: the total over the full term (what they pay, or what they are due to be paid), and the money at stake if it ends early (what leaving costs them, or what they could go unpaid). Show the sum. Work out the calendar date of any notice deadline. If it depends on a date the document does not fix (the day they sign, a renewal date), say which date you assumed. Say the date is your count, that the document may count days differently, and that they should give notice well before it, in the way the document requires (in writing, to a named address).
4. **Findings, most serious first.** At most about eight; group small ones. A finding may rest on two or three clauses that work together; quote each briefly. An unclear clause is itself a finding: give both readings and ask which is meant. Mark each:
   - **High**: could cost a lot for this person and this deal, is hard to undo, or limits their home, income or future work.
   - **Medium**: one-sided with a limited cost, or serious but outranked by the Highs above.
   - **Low**: worth knowing, unlikely to hurt.
   Keep High for the three or four that matter most, in order, so the label keeps its force; if more would qualify, say in the verdict that the agreement is one-sided throughout. If a serious term is also common and rarely changed (such as joint tenants each being answerable for all of the rent), say that it is common and often not changed, that they may still ask, and what to know before accepting it.
   For each: a quote of 20 words or fewer with its clause, what it means in plain words, why it matters to this person, and what to do (ask, change, accept knowingly).
   After the last finding, add one or two lines headed "In your favour" on what is fair or good for them; if nothing is, say so.
5. **Missing.** What an agreement of this type normally has and this one lacks (list below), plus documents referred to but not provided. Six lines at most. If nothing notable, say so.
6. **Questions to ask the other side.** Short and specific, most important first, five at most. Do not repeat what the change requests below already ask.
7. **Suggested changes, ready to send.** A short polite message the person can paste, listing each change as "Clause X: change '...' to '...'", with a one-line reason. Cover the High and Medium findings and any missing term that matters. When there are more than about six changes, quote old and new wording for the High ones and give the rest as one line each: the clause and the effect wanted. Where a change needs a number the document and the person did not give (days of notice, a fee cap), propose a moderate one, and say in the line before the message, and again after it, that these figures are your starting suggestions for them to adjust before sending.
   If the terms cannot be negotiated (terms of service, mass-market subscriptions), skip this and give **Your choices** instead: settings or opt-outs the document itself offers and their deadlines, when to set a cancel reminder, and what to weigh in deciding whether to accept.
8. **Before you sign.** Only the lines that apply: get spoken promises written in; fill or strike blanks; ask for the documents referred to; it is fine to ask for a day to read it; keep a signed copy.
9. **When a lawyer is worth paying for.** Say so, once and specifically, when the agreement involves large sums, buying or selling property, limits on future work, giving up rights or claims, a personal guarantee, or anything hard to undo. Weigh the likely fee against the money at stake: for a small deal, one line saying a lawyer is optional is enough. Where money is tight, mention free routes: tenant, worker or consumer advice services, legal aid, a bar association referral line.

## What is normally there, by type

Use for step 5. These are common practice. Whether the law where the person lives requires any of them is not something this skill states.

- **Lease:** rent, due date and late fee; start and end; deposit amount and how it is returned; all other fees; who repairs what and how to ask; landlord's entry and notice; ending early and subletting; rent on renewal; rules on pets, guests, smoking. Where there are joint tenants, whether each can be held responsible for all of the rent.
- **Freelance or contractor:** exact scope and deliverables; number of revisions; deadlines; rate, invoicing and when payment is due; late-payment fee; expenses; who owns the work and when ownership passes; payment if the client cancels; how either side can end it.
- **Offer letter or employment terms:** role and start date; pay and when it is paid; hours and place; bonus or equity and whether it is guaranteed; leave and benefits; notice on each side; probation; anything promised in interviews. Restrictions after leaving and repayment terms are often in a separate document: ask for it before accepting.
- **Service or subscription:** price now and after any trial or promotion; billing date; minimum term; how and by when to cancel; refunds; what happens if the service fails.
- **Terms of service:** how terms can change and how notice is given; what happens to their data and content; ending or suspending the account; dispute terms and any opt-out window.
- **NDA:** what counts as confidential; one-way or both ways; how long it lasts; the usual exclusions (already known, already public, independently developed, required by law); return or deletion of material; no extra duties hidden in it, such as non-compete or ownership of ideas.

## Example

User: "My landlord sent this, I'm meant to sign by Friday. Anything I should worry about?" followed by a lease. It includes "12. This lease renews automatically for a further 12 months unless Tenant gives 90 days' written notice" and "17. Tenant is responsible for all repairs to the premises."

The reply opens: "This is a 12-month home lease, read from your side as the tenant. This is a plain-language review, not legal advice." The verdict names two issues, and because of the Friday deadline "If you only do three things" follows. The table shows rent $1,800 a month (clause 3), total over the term $21,600, deposit $1,800 (clause 5), renewal automatic with 90 days' notice (clause 12), early exit "not stated". Then:

> **High. Automatic renewal with long notice (clause 12).** "renews automatically for a further 12 months unless Tenant gives 90 days' written notice". If you miss the date, about nine months in, the clause would have you owe another year: $21,600. (The date is my count; give notice well before it.) Ask for 30 days' notice, or month-to-month after the first year. Set a reminder either way.
>
> **High. All repairs fall on you (clause 17).** "Tenant is responsible for all repairs to the premises". As written this covers the roof, heating and plumbing. Rules on what a landlord can pass to a tenant differ by place; your local tenant advice service can tell you. Ask to limit it to damage you cause.

Then "In your favour: the rent is fixed for the year." Missing: how the deposit is returned, notice before the landlord enters. A line says the figures in the message are starting suggestions to adjust, and the message begins: "Thanks for sending the lease. Before I sign, could we make two changes? Clause 12: change '90 days' to '30 days'..."
