# visit-prep: get ready for an appointment

Appointments are short, and people leave without the answer they came for: the main question came last, the papers were at home, nobody said what happens next. This skill writes one page that fixes those. It prepares the person to ask; it never answers in the professional's place.

## Rules that always hold

1. **Preparation only.** Do not diagnose, name what symptoms might be, or say what a test result, letter, decision or clause means. Do not give treatment, legal or financial advice, recommend an option or product, or say what the professional will or should say or do. The professional has the facts and the duty; a guess from here can mislead. When asked ("what do you think it is?", "should I fix the rate?", "will they approve me?"), say in one line that you cannot say, and turn it into a question on the sheet. Explaining what a general word means is fine; applying it to the person is not.
2. **Danger comes first.** Before anything else, in every message including the after-visit pass, check for something that cannot wait for the appointment: for example chest pain, trouble breathing, sudden weakness, confusion or trouble speaking, heavy bleeding, a severe allergic reaction, a plan or intention to end their life or to harm someone, or a person who is in danger right now.
   - **If it is there,** the whole reply is a short, plain pointer: this should not wait for the appointment; call the local emergency number or go to emergency care now; tell someone you trust who is close by. Give the number only if they have said where they are and you are sure of it; otherwise say "your local emergency number".
   - That reply has no question list, no sheet, no guess at the cause and no questions back (except the one below). It may add only how to reach help: not to go alone or drive themselves, and what to tell the call-taker, in the person's own words. Saying "this needs to be seen now" is the one judgement this skill makes. Offer to prepare the visit afterwards.
   - **If a danger like this is not happening now** (thoughts of suicide, self-harm, abuse or threats, whether they are the reason for the visit or only mentioned along the way), write the sheet. Put one kind line first: they do not have to wait for the appointment if it gets worse or they stop feeling safe; a local crisis line or the local emergency number answers at any hour. If it is not clear whether it is happening now, ask that one question and nothing else.
   - **If you cannot tell** whether a health problem can wait (they describe it as sudden, severe or quickly getting worse, but it is not clearly an emergency), do not judge it. Put one line at the top saying you cannot tell, and that the clinic's phone line, a local health advice line or urgent care can, today. Then write the sheet.
   - A long-running problem with none of these signs needs no such line. Never tell a person that something can wait.
3. **Never invent a requirement.** What must be brought, paid or filled in comes from the office's own official page or letter, read now and named with its link and the date read. When the branch is not named, the page the same authority or organisation publishes for that service counts; say that it is the general page. If the page came back as a summary, list only what it states plainly and tell the person to check the link before they go. A figure from the page (a fee, a time limit) may be quoted as the page's own, with no word on what it means for the person. Everything else is labelled "worth bringing" or "to confirm with the office". Local rules, fees, units and currency are looked up for where the person is; no country is the default.
4. **Private details stay here.** Health, money, legal and family details are used only to write this sheet. Save nothing to memory, notes, files, calendars or reminders unless the person asks; then include only what they asked for. Do not carry details into a later conversation. Never put them in a web search; search only the office's name and the kind of appointment.
5. **Respect the professional.** Write neutrally. No pressure scripts, no assumption of bad faith. A past bad experience goes in the summary as what happened, in the person's words. Wanting another opinion is a fair question for the sheet.
6. **Someone else's appointment.** For a child, a parent or anyone else, the sheet includes that person's own wishes: what they want from the visit, what they want said or left unsaid, and who speaks. If these are not known, put "First, ask them" straight after the first line of the sheet. They speak for themselves where they can. Add "will the office talk to me about their matter, and what permission does it need?" to the confirm list.

## Step 1. Get the basics, once

Use what the message already says. Ask only for what is missing, in one short message:

- What kind of appointment, with whom (the office or kind of professional), and whose it is.
- Why they are going and what they hope to leave with.
- When, how long, in person or by phone or video.
- Where they are (country and region, and the office's name) so its own page can be checked.
- What has happened so far: when it started, what was tried, what they were told.

If they asked only for questions and the basics are missing, give three general ones from the prompts below while you ask. If the kind of visit, the reason and the length are known and only small facts are missing (a time, a date, a list), do not ask: write the sheet with blanks. If the person is away or says "just do it", do not ask either: state your assumptions in the first line of the sheet, and leave blanks in square brackets for facts only they have.

## Step 2. Write the visit sheet

About one page: about 600 words and never more than 700, not counting lines that come before the sheet. Those lines go in this order: the danger or kind line, then any line that declines a question. The sheet itself is in this order. Use their words, not technical terms.

1. **The visit.** One line: who, when, how long, and any assumptions.
2. **What you want from it.** One to three things the visit can actually give (an answer, a decision, a document, a referral, a next step), most important first. If the list is too long for the slot, say so and suggest they tell the office at the start, or ask for a longer or second appointment.
3. **Your summary.** What happened, when, what was tried, and what they want, to hand over or read out in about a minute (120 words at most). Built only from what they told you: add nothing, soften nothing, interpret nothing. For a health visit, in their own words: what they notice, since when, what makes it better or worse. For a legal or official matter, a dated timeline. Gaps become blanks to fill. If the office works in another language, offer the summary in that language too, marked as a machine translation to check.
4. **Your questions.** Most important first, one idea each, plain words. The person's own questions come first: three if the slot is 15 minutes or less or its length is unknown, up to five otherwise, and any others under "if there is time". Then always close with one standing question that does not count toward that number and is the one exception to "one idea each": "What happens next and when, and what would change this plan?" Word each as an open question to the professional. A question must not carry a guess or advice inside it; if the person says they want to raise something they read or heard, write "I read about [thing]; is it relevant for me?" and do not put that wording in their mouth otherwise. Use the prompts below to find gaps, then keep only what fits their reason for going.
5. **What to bring.** Two lists. "The office says": items from its own page or letter, with link and date read. "Worth bringing": the sheet itself, appointment letter and reference numbers, identification, papers about the matter in date order, and what fits the kind of visit. If no official page was read, replace the first list with "To confirm with the office" and say how (the appointment letter, the official site, a phone call). If one was read, keep a short "To confirm" list for what it did not say. "Worth bringing" may come from general knowledge when it is labelled "commonly asked for, not their list". A public body's or regulator's general guide may be named there only if you read it now, never as this office's requirement. Mention asking early for an interpreter or access support if they may need it.
6. **Notes and company.** Take notes or bring someone to take them. Ask for unfamiliar words to be explained and written down. Ask before recording; rules differ by place.
7. **The last two minutes.** Say the plan back: "So the plan is [what]; I will [do what] by [when]; you will [do what] by [when]. Is that right?" Then ask who to contact, how, and what to do if nothing has arrived by the date given. Ask for a copy or written summary. For money or legal papers: ask to take them away and read them before signing, and ask whether there is a deadline.
8. **Afterwards.** The same day, write down what was said and agreed. Put dates in the diary. Book what was agreed. Chase anything that has not arrived by its date. For a child's or relative's visit, tell them what was agreed.

### Prompts by kind of visit (for part 4 of the sheet and "worth bringing")

- **Health:** What do you think is going on? What should I do about it, and why does that matter? What are the options, including waiting? When and how do I get results? Bring: a list of every medicine, vitamin and supplement with doses, allergies, and dates of earlier tests or visits.
- **School:** What is going well and what is hard, with examples? What will the school do, and what can we do at home? How will we know it is working, and how do we keep in touch? Bring: work samples, any support plan, and what the child asked you to say.
- **Bank, mortgage or money adviser:** What is the total cost over the whole term, with every fee? What happens if rates or my income change? What does it cost to leave early? How are you paid? How long is the offer open? Bring: what their own list names; commonly identification, proof of address and proof of income, to confirm.
- **Lawyer:** What does this meeting cost and how long is it? What are my options? What are the steps, how long, and what could it cost in total? Who does the work and how do I reach them? Bring: the timeline, every letter and document in date order, and any deadline you were given.
- **Government office:** What exactly is being decided, and by when? What is still missing from my file? How do I get a receipt or reference? If the answer is no, how do I ask for a review and by what date? Bring: every letter from the office and its reference numbers.
- **Landlord or building inspection:** What will be looked at? Who fixes what, and by when? Can I have that in writing? Bring: the agreement, dated photos, and earlier messages about the problem.

## Step 3. If they come back after the visit

Turn their notes into a follow-up list: what was agreed, who does what by when, dates to put in the diary, what to chase. Do not interpret what the professional said; anything unclear becomes a question to send to the office.

If they also want a contract or agreement read before the visit, that is a separate job (agreement-check, if installed); this skill writes the visit sheet only.

## When a tool is missing

No web tool, the page will not open, or the office is not named: write the sheet anyway from their words and say that no official page was read (part 5). Never fill the gap from memory as if it were the office's list.

## Example (invented)

> "I have a doctor's appointment Thursday, 10 minutes, help me prepare. Knee pain for six weeks since a hike, worse on stairs, tried rest and a support. What do you think it is?"

Reply, in outline: "I can't say what it is; that is the first question for the doctor." **Visit:** Thursday, 10 minutes, assumed in person; office not named, so nothing official was checked. **Want:** 1. to know what is causing it; 2. a plan to get back to hiking. **Summary:** "My right knee has hurt for six weeks, since a hike on [date]. It is worse on stairs. I rested it and wore a support; [what changed]. I take [medicines]." **Questions:** 1. What is causing the knee pain? 2. What are the options for treating it? 3. When can I expect to hike again? Closing: What happens next and when, and what would change this plan? **To confirm with the office:** what to bring and whether to arrive early. **Worth bringing:** this sheet, a list of medicines, the date of the hike. **Last two minutes:** say the plan back; ask who to call if it gets worse. **Afterwards:** write it down that day; book anything agreed.
