# event-plan: get the most from an event you are attending

Most people arrive at a conference with a full timetable and no aim, then go home with a bag of cards and no next step. This skill turns the event into two or three goals and builds everything else around them. It is for one attendee at a conference, summit, trade show, offsite or large networking event, in person or online.

## Rules that always hold

- **Never invent.** Every session, speaker, room, stand and time in the plan comes from the agenda the user pasted or a page you opened. No agenda, no session plan (see step 2). A made-up session costs the user a real hour. Times you add yourself (badge, walks, breaks, notes, a meal or a session end the agenda does not time) are marked "suggested"; say once that they are yours, not the agenda's.
- **Date the agenda.** Write "Agenda as read on [date], from [pasted text or page name]" at the top, and tell the user to check it again the day before, because programmes and floor plans change. If you could not read one (no tool, a refused or failed call, not published yet), write "Agenda not read: [why], tried on [date]" instead.
- **Public and professional only.** About any person, use only what the event's own pages and their public professional work say (their talk, role, organisation, published work). Nothing about their private life; if a lookup returns such material, ignore it and do not repeat it. Do not guess a person's gender or title from a name; use the name. Use a search result only when the name and the organisation both match the agenda. Label each fact: (agenda), (you said) or (source: page name, date read). Do not work out what an exhibitor sells or what a talk covers from its name alone; put it on the To do list.
- **Speakers and exhibitors, not attendee lists.** Choose people from the public speaker, exhibitor, sponsor and organiser lists and from who the user says they know. Do not collect, scrape or copy attendee lists, and do not guess email addresses. If the event has its own opt-in meeting tool or app, the user can use it themselves.
- **Drafts only, one person at a time.** Write at most five meeting requests (follow-up drafts in step 10 are counted separately), each to one named person for a stated reason. Never send, book or post anything; never write a message meant to be sent to many people. One polite reminder at most; no answer or a no ends it.
- **Code of conduct.** Tell the user once to read the event's code of conduct and note who to contact. Plan nothing that breaks it: no pressing people who decline, no approaching people in private spaces, and social events count as professional settings. Where one side holds something the other wants, such as a job, keep the ask small and take the first no. Respect signs that forbid photographing or sharing slides.
- **Work rules are the user's.** If they are going for an employer, say once that its travel, expense, gift and confidentiality rules are theirs to follow; if they are between jobs and will talk about a former employer, say once that any confidentiality duty to it still holds. Either way, keep anything confidential they paste out of web searches and out of the drafts.

## Step 1. Get the basics

Take from the message whatever it already gives:

- **The event:** name, dates, place or online, and the agenda (a link or pasted text).
- **Their goals** in their own words: learn, meet people, find customers, a job, partners.
- **Limits:** days attending, arrival and departure, budget, energy, access or food needs, fixed commitments, whether they are speaking or on a stand. A fixed commitment (a train, a call) rules out anything that overruns it; a food or access need goes on the To do list as "tell the organisers", since agendas rarely cover it.
- **Who they are** in one line, and **who they already know** there.

If neither a link nor a pasted agenda was given, or the goals are missing, ask once, in one short message of at most four questions. Do not ask for what you can assume. If the user is away or cannot reply, do not ask: plan on stated assumptions and list them at the top.

For an event in a country the user does not live in, look up local business customs (greetings, cards, punctuality) on a named page if you have a web tool, or put them on the To do list. Never guess customs from a name or nationality.

## Step 2. Read the agenda

Use pasted text first. Given only a link and a web tool, open the event's own agenda, speaker and exhibitor pages (a handful of pages, not the whole site). Note the time zone the times are in; give all times in the venue's time zone.

If the page will not open, there is no web tool, or the agenda is not out yet, say so plainly and do not fill the gap from memory or from search snippets. Do this even when the user is present: give the short plan now and ask for the pasted agenda in the To do list, not as a question that holds up the reply. Then give steps 3, 7, 9 and 10 in full and a short form of the rest: for step 4 the choosing rules in a few lines; for step 5 four to six kinds of people with a question by role; for step 6 up to three templates with [name] and [session] blanks, each to be filled in for one named person (the limit of five requests still holds); for step 8 the shape of a day without times. Keep this reply to about 1,000 words, using the lower numbers. Kinds of people need no source label.

End with a short **To do** list whenever something could not be read or looked up: paste the agenda here, what to check on the event's own pages, anything to ask the organisers.

## Step 3. Two or three goals

Turn what they want into two or three goals they can check on the last day. Each names a number or a result: "three conversations with people who buy lab software", "an answer to whether to move to supplier X", "one follow-up call booked with a hiring manager". If they gave one vague aim ("network"), offer concrete versions and pick the likeliest. Keep to three at most so each can be checked on the last day; say which you dropped.

## Step 4. Sessions

- Mark each chosen session **must** or **if free** (it serves a goal, but less), with the goal it serves. At most three musts a day. A session that serves no goal is left out, however famous the speaker; its slot becomes open.
- **Clashes:** pick one and say why in a line. If both serve the same goal, pick the speaker who cannot be found elsewhere in the programme and put the other speaker on the people list. For the one not chosen, say what to do: watch the recording if the event offers one (check, do not assume), ask for the slides, swap notes with a colleague, or find the speaker afterwards.
- **Gaps on purpose:** leave at least one session slot (a plenary counts; breaks and meals do not) **open** each half-day, meaning no session is planned: it is for booked meetings, stands, corridor conversations or rest. If a half-day has a single session slot and it is a must, leave an hour unplanned instead. Many of the most useful conversations happen outside the rooms, and a full timetable leaves no room for them.
- Flag sessions that need booking or have limited places.
- Tell the user once that they may leave a session that is not serving a goal.
- **Trade show:** in place of sessions, list stands to visit by hall or area in walking order, with what to ask at each; mark up to six stands a day must. Talks at the show follow the session rules above. **Offsite:** the agenda is mostly fixed, so put the effort into the people, the breaks and the follow-up.

## Step 5. People worth meeting

List five to eight for an event of two or three days, three or four for one day: named speakers, exhibitors and organisers from the public lists, people the user already knows, and kinds of people where no name is public ("someone who runs returns at a mid-size retailer"). For each give:

- **Why**, tied to a goal.
- **Where to find them:** their session or stand, from the agenda.
- **An opening question** only they can answer, about their talk or work, not one a search would answer. No flattery lines.

With a web tool, make at most one or two lookups per named person (up to ten in all) on name plus organisation. Without one, write from the agenda and add what to look up to the To do list. People the user already knows come first, for a short catch-up and for introductions.

## Step 6. Meeting requests

Draft three to five messages for the people where a booked slot matters most; fewer if fewer people are named. A request to a stand whose contact is not public may carry a [name] blank, with finding the name on the To do list. Each under about 80 words: who the user is in a line, why this person in particular, a small ask (fifteen minutes at a named break or at their stand), and an easy way to decline. Address each person by the name as the agenda prints it (someone the user knows, as the user names them); never guess a title, gender or form of address from a name. To someone the user already knows, a two-line note without the self-introduction. Suggest sending one to two weeks ahead and give the dates of that window, or now if the event is within two weeks, through the event's meeting tool, a professional profile or a published work address. The user edits and sends each one themselves.

## Step 7. One-line introduction

One spoken line of about twenty words: what they do and for whom, then what they are looking for at this event. Use only what the user said about their work; add no claim about results they did not make. Give one version per goal if the goals differ. Then one question to ask in return, so the talk is two-way.

## Step 8. A plan for each day

For each day give a timeline in the venue's time zone:

- Badge pick-up early on day one; musts, open slots and booked meetings.
- **Walking time** between rooms or halls. Use the venue map if you have it; if not, allow 10 to 15 minutes in a large venue and say it is an allowance.
- **Meals as fixed appointments**, with water, and one real break each half-day away from the floor.
- At most one evening event a day, chosen for a goal.
- **Ten minutes at the end of the day** to finish notes and send anything promised.

Leave travel to the city, the hotel and packing out; say so in a line if they ask. If the user asks for calendar entries, list them (title, time, place) and add them only on a clear yes. Online event: drop walking time, convert times to the user's own zone if they have said it (otherwise keep the agenda's zone and name it), and plan chat or question-and-answer in place of corridors.

## Step 9. Capture

One note per conversation, written within minutes: **who** (name, role, organisation), **where** met, **what** was said that matters, **promised** (by either side), **next step and date**. Any notes app, or the back of their card. For sessions, three lines at most: one idea, one thing to try, one person to ask.

## Step 10. The week after

- **Within two working days:** send what was promised and a short personal message to the top contacts, recalling the conversation.
- **Within a week of the last day:** the remaining contacts; a connection request with a note that says where they met; a 15 to 20 minute call proposed only where there is a reason.
- **Once:** check the goals against what happened, share three lessons with colleagues, and set a reminder to check in with the best contacts in about three months with something useful to them.

Give two or three drafts with square-bracket blanks for what will only be known afterwards.

## The reply

In this order: assumptions and agenda date; goals; sessions; people; meeting requests; introduction; days; capture; week after; To do list if any. Tables for sessions and days. Size: about 1,000 words for a one-day event, up to about 1,700 for two or three days. The counts and timings in this skill are rules of thumb; say so if the user asks why. If it runs over, cut if-free sessions, then people and drafts down to the lower number, never a whole step.

**Before you reply, check:** no session, speaker, room, stand or agenda time that is not in the agenda, and your own times marked suggested; agenda dated; every named person labelled; open slots present; no more than five meeting requests; reminders (code of conduct, work rules) said once.

## Worked example (invented names)

> "I'm going to the Larchfield Packaging Summit next week, help me plan it. I sell labelling machines for a small firm and want customers. Agenda pasted below." (Two days; the pasted agenda includes: Day 1 10:00 keynote, Hall A; 11:30 "Cutting changeover time", Odalys Brenninkmeyer of Tindle Foods, Room 2; 11:30 "Recycled films", Room 4; 14:00 buyer round tables, Hall B, booking needed.)

Shortened reply; a real one covers both days and all ten steps.

- **Agenda as read on [today's date], from your pasted text.** Check it again the day before. Read the event's code of conduct; your firm's travel and expense rules are yours to follow.
- **Goals:** 1. Six conversations with people who run packing lines. 2. Two demo visits booked. 3. Learn what buyers ask about changeover.
- **Day 1 sessions:** 11:30 "Cutting changeover time", Room 2: must (goals 1 and 3). It clashes with "Recycled films": skipped, serves no goal. 14:00 buyer round tables: must (goal 1), **book now**. 10:00 keynote: serves no goal, so the slot is open. After the 14:00 round tables: open.
- **People:** Odalys Brenninkmeyer, Tindle Foods (agenda). Why: speaks on changeover time (goal 3); Tindle Foods may be the kind of site you sell to, and the agenda gives no role, so check both (To do). Where: Room 2 after 11:30. Ask: "What was the hardest part of the changeover work to get accepted?" Also: anyone at the round tables who runs a line with frequent label changes.
- **Meeting request (draft, yours to send):** "Hello Odalys Brenninkmeyer, I work on labelling machines at [firm] and will be at your talk on changeover time. Could I have ten minutes afterwards to ask what was hardest about the changeover? If the day is too full, no problem at all."
- **Introduction:** "I sell labelling machines for a small firm, and I'm here to meet people who run packing lines."
- **Day 1** (suggested times are mine, not the agenda's): 09:15 badge (suggested); 10:00 open; 11:15 walk to Room 2 (suggested allowance); 11:30 must; lunch as a fixed appointment (the pasted agenda gives no time: suggested 12:30); 14:00 round tables; then open; ten minutes for notes before leaving.
- **Then:** the note template, the week-after plan with drafts such as "It was good to meet you at [place]. You mentioned [point]. As promised, here is [item].", and a To do list (the speaker's role and what Tindle Foods makes; whether talks are recorded).
