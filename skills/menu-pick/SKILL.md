# menu-pick

A menu is written to sell, and the person reading it usually has two minutes, a hungry table and one chance to order. This skill reads the menu they actually have and turns it into an order they can say out loud, with the safety questions that only the staff can answer.

## Rules that never bend

1. **Allergies are safety, not preference.** A menu description can show that an allergen is in a dish; it can never show that one is absent, because sauces, stock, garnishes, oil and shared fryers are rarely printed and menu rules differ by country. So never write that a dish is "safe", "fine", "free from" or "does not contain" an allergen, and never rank dishes as "safest", even when the person asks which ones are safe. Sort instead into two groups, and give both in the reply:
   - **Looks risky from the description:** the allergen is named, or the dish is one that usually has it, or has an unnamed sauce, dressing, topping or assortment of a kind that often does (say which and why).
   - **Not obvious from the description, still confirm:** every other dish. Say in the reply that this is not a clearance. A dish that only sometimes has the allergen goes here with a note, and so does a shared wok, grill or fryer, as a question.

   Then, in every reply that involves an allergy:
   - **The staff lines, near the top:** tell the staff about the allergy before ordering, every time, also when ordering by QR code, app or phone; ask them to check with the kitchen; if staff cannot confirm or seem unsure, do not order that dish. Say once that even a kitchen that confirms a dish cannot promise nothing has touched it.
   - **Printed marks** ("nut-free", "GF", an allergen icon) are the restaurant's claim: report them as "the menu marks this as ...; confirm with staff". If the mark names a different or wider allergen than the person's (a "nut-free" mark and a peanut allergy), ask whether it covers theirs. A dish that names a related but different allergen (cashew, for a peanut allergy) is a question for the person, not an entry in the risky group (if they cannot reply: "if they also avoid it, or are not sure, leave it").
   - **The dish you propose for the allergic person** comes from the "not obvious" group, is chosen for taste and fit, and is called "the one to ask about first", never the safest. Do not give a printed mark as the reason for choosing it. Name a second dish to ask about, and if the kitchen confirms neither, they ask what the kitchen can prepare for them.
   - **Own plate:** suggest the allergic person has their own plate instead of eating from shared dishes, since shared plates and spoons spread allergens. Their plate counts as their main; size the shared order for the others and, where the menu allows, keep risky-group dishes off the shared table.
   - **No medical advice.** The one practical line: they carry any medication prescribed to them, and if they react, they follow the plan their doctor gave them and call the local emergency number.
2. **Dietary and religious rules stand as the person states them.** Do not judge, loosen or explain them back. Do not widen a rule beyond what they said; if a nearby point might matter to them (how meat is slaughtered, vinegar made from wine), raise it once as a question (if they cannot reply, as one "if you also avoid ..., ask about ..." line). An ingredient they avoid, alcohol included, is handled here, not under rule 5. Sort dishes into the same two groups as rule 1 ("looks to break the rule from the description", "not obvious, still ask") and point out the hidden ingredients worth asking about (see Step 3). Do not vouch that a dish or a restaurant meets a rule such as halal, kosher or Jain; say what to ask or which certification to look for.
3. **Only the menu provided.** Recommend only dishes that are on it, with prices as printed. Never invent a dish, a price, an ingredient or a portion size. A description of what a dish "usually" is must be labelled as usual, not as this kitchen's recipe. If part of the menu cannot be read, say which part.
4. **No calorie counting, diet coaching or health claims.** If asked what is "healthiest" or "lightest", describe how dishes are cooked and how filling they are likely to be, and leave the judgement to them.
5. **Alcoholic drinks are suggested only if the person asks**, and then always with a non-alcoholic option beside them.
6. **Opinions are labelled.** Anything said about the restaurant is either from a named public source with its date, or labelled "my reading of the menu". No reputation from memory. A general remark about a cuisine ("a classic of the region") is labelled as general knowledge, not as praise of this kitchen.
7. **No country is the default.** Use the currency and units on the menu. Service charge, tax, cover charge and tipping custom differ by place: look them up if you know where the person is and have a search tool, naming the source; otherwise mark them "to check".

## Step 1. Get the menu and the table

If there is no menu yet, ask for it (photo, link or pasted text) and stop; do not recommend from what the place "probably" serves. If a link cannot be opened or a photo cannot be read, say so and ask for pasted text or a clearer photo; do not retry a refused page.

Then ask, in one short message, only for what the request has not already answered:

- How many are eating, and anyone to plan around (children, a big or small eater).
- Allergies, and any dietary or religious food rules.
- Sharing dishes or one plate each.
- How hungry, and a budget if there is one (per person or for the table).
- What they feel like or want to avoid; the occasion.
- Where they are (city and country), if a total with service or tax matters to them.

**If it is not clear whether they can reply** (a pasted menu and one line): give the order with stated assumptions, the allergy line below, and the questions that would change it at the end as "tell me if ...".

**If the person is at the table, in a hurry, away, or says "just tell me":** ask nothing. Give the order now, state your assumptions in one line at the top, keep the allergy box, the order, the staff questions and the total in full, cut every other part to a line or two, and put the allergy box and the order first. Because they cannot come back to you, give a fallback from the menu for any dish that hangs on a staff answer. A fallback for the allergic person is itself a dish to ask about; say so. If allergies were not mentioned write "Allergies: not told to me. If anyone has one, tell the staff before ordering."

## Step 2. Read the menu

Look for what this kitchen is likely best at. The useful signs are structural:

- What the place calls itself, and the section with the most depth or care.
- A technique or piece of equipment that recurs (a grill, a wood oven, a tandoor, house-made pasta, a named supplier or region).
- A short, focused menu suggests each dish is made often. A very long one covering many cuisines suggests some dishes are made rarely.
- Dishes far from the theme (the pasta at a grill house) are usually there for the guest who wants nothing else. They are a reasonable skip, not a bad dish.

Menus are designed to steer: boxes, photos, first and last places in a list, and long adjectives show what the restaurant wants to sell, which is evidence neither for nor against the dish. A very expensive item can be there to make its neighbours look fair. "Market price" and unpriced specials need a question before ordering.

If the restaurant is named and you have a search tool, make at most three or four lookups for what public reviewers say it does well. Use only what you read, name the source and date, and say when you found nothing. Without a tool, say the reading comes from the menu alone.

## Step 3. Build the order

**Size it.** Starting points, to adjust for appetite and to confirm with staff, because portion sizes are not on the menu:

- Sharing: about one main-sized dish per person, plus rice, bread or noodles for the table. A starter or two for the table comes on top only for a hungry group. Where the whole menu is small plates or tapas: two to three per person. For a light meal take one dish away; for a hungry table add one.
- One plate each: a main per person; starters at about one for every two people to share; desserts shared unless they want their own.
- Children: a shared portion or a starter as a main, unless there is a children's list.

**Balance it.** Across the table, mix a main ingredient or two, a vegetable dish and a staple; vary the cooking (not all fried, not all creamy); include one plain, familiar dish for a cautious eater and one thing the kitchen seems proud of. Everyone must have enough to eat within their own rules, not only side dishes.

**Check each chosen dish** against every allergy and rule at the table. Places allergens and animal products commonly hide, to raise as questions and never as facts about this kitchen: stock and broth; sauces, dressings, pastes and marinades (for example fish sauce, shrimp paste, oyster sauce, pesto, satay, tahini, Worcestershire sauce); garnishes and toppings; anything that arrives unchosen (bread, a cover dish, a side); cooking fat (lard, butter, ghee, nut or sesame oil); the shared fryer and grill; gelatine, rennet in cheese and egg in pasta, noodles, glazes and batters; alcohol in sauces and desserts; last-minute substitutions.

**Fit the budget** using printed prices only. If the order is over, name the swap that brings it under.

## Step 4. Write the reply

Short enough to read at the table: aim for under about 400 words in a hurry and 600 otherwise, not counting part 1; if over, cut parts 2, 5 and 6 first (but when they asked what the dishes are, part 5 stays whole and is not counted). Use these parts, in this order (in a hurry, part 3 moves above part 2), and leave out any that do not apply:

1. **Allergy and food-rule box** (if any): who, what, the staff lines, and both groups from rule 1: the dishes on the whole menu that look risky from the description, and a line that every other dish is "not obvious, still confirm", which is not a clearance.
2. **What this kitchen looks best at:** two to four lines, labelled as your reading or with named sources.
3. **The order for the table:** dish names exactly as printed, the quantity, the price, one reason each, and a marker for any allergy or rule question. Every dish the allergic person will eat carries a marker, at least "confirm"; no unmarked row for them. Then **a per-person option**: what each person would order if not sharing, or for the person with a restriction, their own plate.
4. **Ask the staff:** today's specials and their prices; portion size ("is this enough for four?"); how a named dish is made; the allergy and food-rule questions, worded so they can be read out.
5. **Terms explained:** unfamiliar dishes and words in one plain line each, labelled as the usual meaning. If they asked what the dishes are, cover the whole menu here.
6. **What to skip and why:** two or three items at most, with a reason drawn from the menu (off the theme, repeats something already ordered, poor value next to a similar dish). Never "skip it, it is unsafe" as a substitute for the allergy box.
7. **Estimated total:** each line as quantity x price, the sum, the per-person share, what is not included (drinks, and unpriced dishes listed as "price to ask"), and service, tax and tip as looked up with a source or "to check" (if looked up, state the custom; work out a tip figure only if asked).
8. **Menu in another language:** put the translations in part 5 (the dishes you recommend and any the person asked about), marking any word you are unsure of instead of guessing. Here give two or three short polite phrases in the local language with a plain reading aid: how to order, how to ask a question from part 4, and a sentence stating any allergy or food rule. Say the wording is not checked by a fluent speaker. A translated allergy sentence is a help, not a guarantee: tell them to show it in writing as well as say it, and for a serious allergy to carry a translated allergy card from an allergy organisation or have the wording checked by a fluent speaker.

A reply that gives an order ends with the one or two things to settle with the staff, not with an open question.

## Follow-ups

If the person comes back with what the staff said (a special, a dish that is off, an answer on ingredients), adjust the order and the total. Check which question each staff answer really covers (an answer about the stock is not an answer about nuts) and say what is still open. A staff answer on an allergen is theirs to weigh: repeat it as "the staff told you ...", keep the rule 1 wording, and do not turn it into your own clearance. It may change which dish you suggest asking about or ordering next; the line that a dish is not ordered when staff seem unsure still stands.

## When it does not fit

- Choosing where to eat: say this skill starts once there is a menu, and help in the ordinary way.
- Placing or paying for the order: this skill never orders. Give the list for them to place.
- A fixed tasting menu with no choices: explain the terms and give the staff questions only.

## Example

> Menu from "Example Grill House" (pasted). Four of us, sharing, fairly hungry, about 100 for the table. My sister is allergic to sesame. Which dishes are safe for her?

The reply, in part:

- **Allergy box:** SESAME (1 person). I cannot tell you any dish is safe for her; a menu does not show everything in a dish. Tell the staff before ordering and ask them to check with the kitchen; if they cannot confirm or seem unsure, do not order that dish, and even a confirmed dish is not a promise that nothing has touched it. She should carry any medication prescribed to her, and if she reacts, follow her doctor's plan and call the local emergency number. Looks risky from the description: Hummus plate (usually made with tahini, which is sesame), Seeded flatbread, Chicken skewers with "house dressing" (unnamed dressings often use tahini or sesame oil). Not obvious from the description, still confirm (not a clearance): every other dish, including the Grilled lamb chops, Charred peppers and Rice. Her own plate, not the shared ones; the one to ask about first is the Grilled lamb chops, and if the kitchen cannot confirm them, the Charred peppers with rice.
- **Reading of the menu:** six of the eleven mains come off the charcoal grill and the menu names its butcher, so the grill is where this kitchen puts its effort (my reading of the menu, no reviews looked up).
- **Order line:** Grilled lamb chops x 2 at 24 = 48. Ask: what are they marinated in, and is the grill shared with the seeded bread?
- **Skip:** the Seafood linguine, the only pasta on a grill menu.
- **Total:** 48 + 14 + 9 + 12 + 6 = 89 for four, about 22 each, before drinks. Service and tax: to check on the bill.

The dishes and prices in this example are invented. Use only what is on the person's menu.
