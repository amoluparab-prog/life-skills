# host-plan

A host's two real risks are making a guest ill and spending the party in the kitchen. This skill plans around both: guests' safety first, then a menu and timeline the host can actually carry out. It suits about 4 to 40 guests where the host cooks or arranges the food.

## Rules that never bend

1. **Allergies are safety, not preference.** Ask about them once, then repeat them in a box at the top of the menu and against every dish. Never write that a dish is "free from" an allergen. Write "no X in the recipe as written; check the label on: ..." and list every bought item in it (stock, sauces, spice mixes, bread, chocolate, bakery items). A "may contain" line counts as "contains" unless the guest says otherwise; such warnings are voluntary, so their absence proves nothing. Cooking does not remove an allergen, and picking it off a plate is not enough. Plan clean hands, surfaces and cloths, separate boards, spoons and serving dishes; the allergic guest's food is prepared first and served first; the host keeps the packaging. The guest (or a child's parent) has the last word on what is safe for them. While allergies are unknown, do not build the menu on nuts, peanuts, sesame or shellfish. Give no medical advice; the one practical line is that if a guest reacts, they use their own prescribed medication if they carry it and the host calls the local emergency number.
2. **Food-safety figures are looked up, not remembered.** How long food may sit out, hot and cold holding temperatures, and how to cool, keep and reheat leftovers differ between countries. Take them from the official food-safety body of the host's country, and name the body and the date you read the page. What you could not read is marked "to check" (Step 2).
3. **Alcohol is optional and never pushed.** Ask once whether the host wants it. Always plan non-alcoholic drinks with the same care, whatever the answer. None at a children's party unless the host asks. If alcohol is served, add one line on how guests get home.
4. **Religious and cultural food rules stand as the host states them.** Do not judge, loosen or explain them back. If you are unsure what a rule covers, ask the host; if the host is away, plan to the stricter reading and list it under the decisions for the host to confirm with those guests. If someone questions a rule, it stays the host's call: plan to it as stated and give no opinion on whether it is needed. Do not vouch that a product meets a rule; say which certification mark or label to look for.
5. **Every quantity shows its working** (per head x heads = total, then rounded to a pack size), so the host can rescale when the guest list changes.
6. **Calendar entries and reminders only if the host asks.** If asked and you have no such tool, give the dated list for them to copy.
7. **No country is the default.** Ask where the host is. Use their units and currency. Prices are estimates unless you looked them up; say which. If the host is away and gave no country, use the units they wrote in, take the "to check" path, give no prices or totals, and list the country as a point to confirm.

## Step 1. Ask once

Send one short batch with only what the request has not already answered:

- The occasion, and the style: sit-down, buffet, or standing with nibbles.
- Date, the time guests arrive, and roughly how long it runs.
- Country and city; home or a hired room (and what that room has and allows).
- How many adults, children and older guests.
- Allergies, diets and anything guests avoid, including religious or cultural food rules.
- Budget.
- Kitchen: oven, number of hob rings, fridge space, big pots, serving dishes; seats and tables.
- How much they want to cook (all, some with bought items, mostly bought, or guests bring dishes) and how confident they are.
- Alcohol or not; music, games or a theme if wanted.

**If the host is away or says "just plan it":** ask nothing. State your assumptions at the top. If allergies are unknown, do not assume there are none: write "ASK YOUR GUESTS" in the allergy box and give a one-line message the host can send them.

**If they only ask how much food or drink to get:** answer that first with Step 3 part 3 (by type of dish if there is no menu yet), state the assumptions, say allergies are not yet known and must be asked, give the safety line, and offer the rest of the plan in one sentence.

## Step 2. Look up

- The official food-safety body for the host's country: time out, holding temperatures, cooling, keeping and reheating leftovers.
- That country's list of allergens that must be declared on labels, because labels there are built on it.
- If the menu has meat, poultry, fish or eggs: the official safe cooking temperature for it.
- Prices only when the budget is tight, and last: safety and allergens come first.

A search title or snippet is not a source for a safety figure; you need to have read the page. If a page is refused, do not retry. A tips page or leaflet on the official body's own site counts; say what kind of page it is, and do not stretch it beyond what it says.

**One figure missing.** If you read some figures but not one the plan needs (a cooking temperature, a hot-holding temperature, how many days leftovers keep), give the ones you read as fact, and for the missing one write "To check: [what], on [the body or page to look at]" and give no number for it.

**The "to check" path.** When no official page could be read at all, or you have no search tool, say so at the top and write: "Cautious working rule, not your country's official figure: do not leave food that belongs in the fridge or on the heat out for more than 2 hours in total, 1 hour in hot weather (about 32 C or more); then throw it away. Check with [the national food-safety body, named if you know it]." Give no other temperatures or keeping times as fact.

These habits hold everywhere and need no lookup: keep hot food hot and cold food cold; put out small platters and replace them instead of topping up; cool leftovers quickly in shallow containers; reheat until steaming hot throughout.

## Step 3. Write the plan

Nine parts in this order. Scale the length to the event: dinner for six needs a page, not a manual.

1. **At a glance.** Occasion, date, headcount, style, assumptions, and the allergy and diet box.
2. **Menu.** Fit the guests first, then the host's skill and kitchen. Every guest gets a full plate, not only side dishes; where you can, make the dish everyone can eat the default. At most one dish that is new to the host and one cooked at the last minute (it may finish after guests arrive if it needs little attention or a guest covers the room). Spread the work over oven, hob, cold and bought. Give a table: dish; made when (days ahead, day before, on the day, last minute); cooked where; allergens in the recipe (those on the country's declared list, and always the guests' own); labels to check for the guests' allergens; who cannot eat it.
3. **Quantities.** A table with the working. Starting points per adult, to adjust and convert to the host's units:
   - Nibbles: 3 to 8 pieces before a meal (fewer when the meal follows soon); 12 to 15 when they are the meal.
   - Main protein: 110 to 170 g cooked boneless when plated, 170 to 225 g at a buffet. Meat loses weight in cooking, so buy about a quarter to a third more raw; 350 to 450 g raw if on the bone. A meat-free main: a full plated portion for each guest relying on it.
   - Each side (rice, potatoes, vegetables): 110 to 170 g cooked, less with three or more sides. Leafy salad: one cupped handful. Bread: 1 or 2 pieces.
   - Dessert: one portion each.
   - Drinks: 1 to 2 glasses per person per hour in total, about half of them water. If wine is served, about half a bottle per drinker for an evening. Ice: 0.5 to 1 kg per person if drinks are served cold.

   Adjust: children count as about half for food (not for drinks); more dishes means less of each; with two mains, count each for the guests likely to take it, not a full portion for everyone, but the main for vegetarian or allergic guests must cover them in full; a long or late event needs more; add 10 to 15 percent to main dishes only (at a snack party, to the dishes that fill people up). Do not multiply every recipe by the headcount.
4. **Shopping list.** Grouped by when to buy and by shop section or type of shop, whichever way the host shops (fresh produce, meat and fish, chilled and dairy, bakery, dry goods and tins, frozen, drinks, non-food such as napkins, foil, bin bags and containers for leftovers). Each line has the quantity from part 3. Buy: a week ahead (dry goods, drinks, non-food), two or three days ahead (most fresh food), the day before or on the day (bread, fish, salad leaves, ice). Mark every item whose label must be checked. Give a rough total against the budget and name the swap if it is over.
5. **Timeline.** Count back from arrival: a week before, two or three days, the day before, the morning, the last two hours, the last hour, during, and after. Include an oven and hob plan: list each dish that needs the oven (setting and time) or a ring, name every clash (two settings at once, more pans than rings, no room in the fridge) and fix it by making ahead and reheating, serving at room temperature, or swapping the dish. Leave a 30-minute buffer and have the host free before guests arrive. Put the food-safety lines where they apply: when dishes go out, when they come off the table, when leftovers go in the fridge. Food carried to a hired room counts its travel as time out.
6. **Drinks.** Per rule 3, with quantities from part 3.
7. **Room.** Where coats go; drinks away from the kitchen door so people do not jam there; seats against headcount; a proper chair for older guests; the allergy-safe dish set apart with its own spoon; music or activities only if wanted; for a children's party, a simple running order.
8. **Jobs for guests.** Specific asks (bring ice, a dessert, run the drinks, take photos). If guests bring dishes and someone has an allergy, ask each to bring the ingredient list or packaging; the allergic guest eats only what has been checked.
9. **The day after.** Leftovers by the official figures (or "to check"), things to return, thank-yous, and one note on what to change next time.

End with the two or three decisions the host still has to make, not with an open question.

## When it does not fit

- More than about 40 guests, or caterers and vendors to manage (a wedding reception, a public event): say this is beyond a home plan, give the quantities part if it helps, and suggest a caterer or planner.
- The steps of a religious ceremony: plan the meal and hosting only (a festival dinner is in scope; the observance itself is not) and point to the tradition's own guidance (or a ritual skill if installed).

## Example

> I'm hosting a farewell dinner for 10 adults at my flat in Melbourne on Saturday, 7 pm. One guest has a peanut allergy, two are vegetarian. Small oven, four rings. Budget 150 dollars. I'll cook the mains and buy dessert. No alcohol.

The reply, in part:

- **Allergy and diet box:** PEANUT ALLERGY (1 guest): treat as safety. Vegetarian: 2.
- **Menu line:** Roast vegetable and chickpea tray bake (main for all, made on the day, oven). No peanut in the recipe as written; check the label on: spice mix, stock cubes, tinned chickpeas. Ask the guest about the bought dessert before buying; keep the box.
- **Quantity line:** Rice: 60 g dry per head (about 150 g cooked) x 10 = 600 g; buy a 1 kg bag. Tray bake, as the main: 10 portions plus 10 percent = 11; make 12, which fills two trays.
- **Oven clash:** Tray bake needs 200 C for 45 minutes and the flatbreads 220 C for 10. Bake the tray first, cover it, then turn the oven up for the bread at 6:40 pm.
- **Food safety:** From Food Standards Australia New Zealand's 2-hour / 4-hour rule (a fact sheet written for food businesses, read today): time between 5 C and 60 C adds up across preparing and serving; under 2 hours the food can go back in the fridge, between 2 and 4 hours use it now, over 4 hours throw it away. So food is served at 7:30 pm and off the table by 9 pm, which leaves room for time out during preparation, and leftovers go in the fridge straight away.
- **Still to decide:** which dessert, and whether a neighbour can lend two chairs.

The figures in this example are an illustration. Never reuse them; read the page in the session.
