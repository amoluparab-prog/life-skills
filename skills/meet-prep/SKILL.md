# meet-prep: a one-page brief before meeting someone

A meeting with one person goes well when the user knows what they want from it, what the other person cares about, and what to say first and last. This skill writes that down on one page. It is for a meeting with one person or a small group (about four people at most).

## Rules that always hold

- **Professional and public only.** Use what the person has made public in their working role. Never look for or include a home address, family, health, age, religion, politics, personal social media accounts (public or not; a professional profile and public posts about their work are fine), or anything unrelated to the meeting, even if the user asks; say why and offer a work-related alternative. If the user volunteers such a detail, keep it out of the brief and the follow-up. Leave out allegations, legal disputes and rumours about the person unless the user raises them and they bear directly on the meeting; then report only what a named source says, as that source's claim. People are owed their private lives, and small talk built on them reads as creepy.
- **Private individuals: the user's words only.** If the person is private as step 2 defines it (not named with a role in a named organisation, or met for a personal reason whatever their job: a neighbour, a private seller, a date, a landlord), do not search for them at all.
- **Never invent.** No made-up fact, role, date, number or quote about anyone, including the user. Where a fact is missing, leave a blank in square brackets for the user to fill.
- **Same name, uncertain match.** Accept a search result only when the name and the organisation both match what the user said; a matching name and job title alone is not enough. If something else on the page conflicts with what the user said (country, seniority, dates), or the name is common and the organisation is large, say the match is uncertain and leave it out.
- **No manipulation.** No flattery lines, pressure tactics, or pretending to share an interest. A shared thing may be mentioned once if it is true and about work. If the user has an interest the other person would want to know about (they may become a competitor, they hope for a job or money), build saying so into the opening.
- **Confidential material.** Use the user's employer or client material only if they paste it, only inside the brief, and never put it into a web search. If the user says the meeting or the other side's name is confidential, do not search on the names of the people or the organisation on the other side.
- **Draft only.** Never send a message, book a meeting or contact anyone.

## Step 1. Get the four basics

Take from the message whatever it already gives: **who** (name, role, organisation, how the user knows them), **why** the meeting is happening and who asked for it, **how long** and in what form (in person, call), and **what the user wants** out of it.

If any are missing, ask for them once, in one short message; if the user asked only for questions, add two or three general ones they can use meanwhile. Do not ask for what you can assume. If the user is away, cannot reply, or the meeting is within a few hours, do not ask: write the brief on stated assumptions (30 minutes if no length is given) and list them at the top.

Ask where the meeting takes place only if the two sides are in different countries or the user says customs worry them. Then look up the local points that matter (how to address the person, punctuality, cards, gifts) and say where they came from, in a short Customs list after the brief; it does not count toward the one page. Where two sources disagree, give both and name each. Never guess customs from a person's name or nationality; if you cannot check, say so.

## Step 2. Find out about the person

First decide from what the user told you, before any search: public professional or private individual? A public professional is someone the user is meeting about work and names with a role in a named organisation (for example an executive, founder or owner, investor, official, academic), or who writes and speaks publicly about their work. Anyone else is private, and so is anyone the user is meeting for a personal reason (a date, a landlord, a family matter, a dispute), whatever their job. When unsure, treat them as private.

For a public professional, if you have a web tool, make up to five lookups in all (for a small group, the decision-maker first): their organisation's own page about them, their professional profile, talks, interviews or articles by them, and recent news from the organisation. Search on the name together with the organisation, never the name alone. Read pages, not search snippets. If a page will not open, label anything taken from its search snippet (unconfirmed) and move on. If two searches bring back no page that matches both name and organisation, stop searching and work from the user's words.

Label every line about the person with one of:
- **(you said)** from the user.
- **(source: page name, link, date read)** from a page you opened. If your tool returns a summary and not the page itself, use only what the returned text states and say it was a summary.
- **(unconfirmed)** from a search snippet or a page that would not open, where both the name and the organisation matched. Anything that did not match both is left out, not labelled.

With no web tool, or nothing found, do not fill the gap. Write the section from the user's words and add a short **To check yourself** list: what to look up and where (for example "their bio on the organisation's site", "their last public talk").

## Step 3. Work out what they care about

Give up to three things the person likely cares about right now, each with how you know: they said it publicly, the user reports it, or it is a guess from their role (for example, a new leader usually wants to learn who does what and where the problems are). Mark guesses as guesses.

## Step 4. Write the brief

Keep it to about one page (roughly 500 to 700 words; a line or two per part), in this order. If you declined something or found nothing, say so in a line or two above the brief. If the brief has square-bracket blanks, tell the user there to fill them before the meeting.

1. **The meeting.** Who, when, how long, why. Assumptions made.
2. **Who they are.** Three to five sourced lines about their professional role. For a small group, one or two lines per person and who makes the decision.
3. **What they likely care about.** From step 3.
4. **Outcome and fallback.** One thing to aim for that the other person can agree to in the room, sized to the time. If what the user wants is more than this meeting is likely to give (a full yes from someone new, a job from a first coffee), say so in one line and aim for the step that leads to it. Then a smaller fallback (for example, a second meeting or a name to talk to).
5. **Opening line.** One or two sentences: a single thank-you, why you are there, a check on the time. No apology for taking their time and no praise, first-hand or passed on.
6. **Questions, best first.** Two for up to 20 minutes, three for up to 40, up to five for longer; time gets cut, so the most important goes first. Ask open questions only this person can answer, not what a search would tell you. Include one about their priorities.
7. **One thing to offer.** Something concrete the user can really deliver (an introduction, a piece of data, a draft, an hour of help). If you do not know what they can offer, ask or leave a blank; never invent an ability.
8. **Hard questions and honest answers.** Two to four questions the person may ask, each with a two-sentence answer built only from the user's facts. Sums done on the user's own numbers are fine if you show them. Where the facts are missing, a blank plus "I don't know yet; I will tell you by [day]" is a good answer. State past decisions as facts, without blaming anyone.
9. **Close.** With about five minutes left (two in a short call): say back what was agreed, who does what by when, and ask if you may follow up.
10. **Follow-up draft.** A short message to send within a day: specific thanks, one thing taken away, the agreed actions, with blanks for what will only be known after the meeting. Tell the user it is theirs to edit and send.

## Notes by kind of meeting

- **New leader or stakeholder:** aim to learn their priorities and how they like to work; bring a short account of what you do, not a list of requests.
- **Skip-level (your manager's manager):** do not go round your manager or criticise people; raise issues as problems with a suggested fix. Telling your manager you are meeting is usually wise.
- **Client:** find out what they are trying to get done before describing anything of yours.
- **Investor, first meeting:** it is two-way getting to know each other, not a full pitch; check what the firm says it invests in; expect sceptical questions on market, how you reach customers and money; aim for a clear next step.
- **Mentor:** bring your own agenda and goals; agree how often and how you will keep in touch.
- **Networking coffee:** it is for learning, not for asking a near stranger for a job; make any referral request specific.

## Before you reply, check

One page. Every line about the person labelled. Nothing private, nothing invented, blanks where facts are missing. Nothing confidential beyond the brief. The follow-up is a draft.

## Worked example (invented names)

> "I'm meeting our new head of operations, Ilse Varga, tomorrow for 30 minutes. She asked to meet all team leads. I run the returns team at Brightwater Depot and I want her backing to trial weekend shifts."

No web tool is available, so the brief uses only what the user said (shortened here; a real brief has all ten parts and two to four hard questions):

- **Who she is:** new head of operations (you said); meeting all team leads (you said). **To check yourself:** her bio on the company intranet; any note announcing her arrival.
- **Likely cares about:** learning who does what and where the problems are (guess from her being new).
- **Outcome:** she agrees to look at a one-page trial plan next week. **Fallback:** she names who should see it first.
- **Opening:** "Thanks for making time. I run returns; I'd like to give you a two-minute picture, raise one idea for a trial, and hear what you most want from us. Is half an hour still right?"
- **Questions:** 1. What do you most want to change in your first three months? 2. What would you like returns to do differently? 3. How do you prefer to get proposals: a page, or a conversation?
- **Offer:** [something you can really deliver, for example a one-page summary of returns volumes by day for the last quarter, if you have the data].
- **Hard question:** "What will weekend shifts cost?" Answer: "About [your estimate] for a four-week trial; I will send the working by [day]."
- **Close:** "So I will send the one-page plan by [day], and you will tell me who else should see it. May I follow up next week?"
- **Follow-up draft:** "Thank you for the time today. What stayed with me was [one point she made]. As agreed, I will send [item] by [day]."
