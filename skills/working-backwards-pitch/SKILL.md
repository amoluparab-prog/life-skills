# Working-backwards pitch

A senior person decides faster when they can see the finished result from the side of the people it serves, the hard questions already answered, and a small, safe thing to say yes to. This skill writes that, using the press release and FAQ ("PR/FAQ") method Amazon made public, cut down for one person pitching upward. It is not affiliated with Amazon.

## Honesty rules (they hold throughout and outrank polish)

A mock press release looks like news. Outside a company where everyone knows the format it can be mistaken for fact, and one invented figure can cost the author their standing.

- **Label it.** The press release carries this line at its top and again at its bottom: `HYPOTHETICAL - written to describe a possible future. Nothing in this press release has happened.` Give it a future date and write "(imagined)" beside the date.
- **Invent nothing that reads as evidence.** No made-up numbers, customers, partners, awards or results. Use only figures the user gave or that you looked up, each with its source. Where a number would go and none exists, write `[to validate: what number, and how to get it]`. Sums worked out from the user's own figures are fine if you show the working. For a name the user did not give, use a blank such as `[Company]`.
- **Design numbers are suggestions.** Numbers that design the proposal (how many weeks, how many people, a threshold) are yours to suggest, each marked `[proposed]`; they are choices, not evidence, and stay off the to-validate list. A target or forecast of a result ("cancellations fall 20%") is not a design number: it never appears as a figure without a source. If the author still wants a goal after you have explained this, write it once, on the one-pager only, as their goal and not a forecast, marked `[to validate]`.
- **Quotes.** No customer testimonial and no quote from the decision-maker or any other real person. The only quote allowed is the author's own: use words they actually gave you, word for word, and ask them to confirm they are happy to be quoted. Never write a quote for them. If they gave no words or are away, leave the quote out. If they ask for someone else's quote or a guessed figure, say no, say why, and offer the real version: ask the people, get the number, and put the true result in as a sourced fact.
- **Mark what is unproven.** Any claim that rests on belief gets `[to validate]`. All of these are collected in a list at the end.
- **Do not oversell.** If the idea has a weak spot, the FAQ and the red-team note say so.

## 0. Check fit

Use this only when the idea has not happened yet and a named person or group will decide on it. If the thing is real and launching, they need a real press release; if they want slides, an investor deck or a sales pitch to a customer, or want the idea itself questioned from scratch (the bedrock skill, if installed): say so and help without this skill. If they ask for one part only, such as the press release, write that part under the same rules with its to-validate list, and offer the rest in one line. If they have no decision-maker in mind yet, ask who could say yes.

## 1. Gather, in one short round

Take what the message already gives. Ask only for what is missing, in a single message, as a short list:

1. The idea: who it serves, what problem they have today, what changes for them.
2. The decision-maker: who they are and what they care about most (growth, cost, risk, reputation).
3. The want: what a yes would give the author (money, people, time, permission, an introduction).
4. Real facts and figures they already have, and where each comes from.
5. One or two sentences on why they believe in it, in their own words (this may become the quote).

Do not ask a second round. If an answer is thin, state an assumption and go on.

**If the user is away, wants it in one go, or cannot reply:** ask nothing and do not end on a question. Draft from what they gave, list your assumptions at the top, leave the quote out, and mark more as `[to validate]`.

If you have a web search tool and the pitch leans on a public fact (a market size, a rule, a competitor), look up a few and cite them. If you have none, do not fill in from memory; mark them `[to validate]`.

## 2. Write the document, in this working order

Work backwards: the press release first, because it forces the benefit to be concrete; the one-pager last.

**a. Press release (under about 350 words, labels and markers aside).** Written as if the idea has worked, for the people it serves, in plain words. Parts: headline naming who benefits and how; one-paragraph summary; the problem today; what the idea does about it; what using it is like, step by step; the author's quote if there is one; how someone gets started. Test it with "so what?": if a person it serves would shrug, the benefit is not clear yet, so rewrite it around the single most important benefit and not a list of features. Date it when the result could first plausibly be seen. Describe results in words, not figures, with `[to validate]` where the story depends on one.

**b. FAQ (6 to 10 questions).** Two or three that the people it serves would ask. The rest are the decision-maker's hard ones (two topics may share one question); always cover: what it costs and who does the work; how you know people want it; what else was considered, including doing nothing; what will disappoint people at first; what it depends on; what could go wrong and whether it can be undone. Answer each in two to four sentences. "Not known yet; we will find out by [how, when]" is a good answer. A confident guess is not.

**c. The sized ask.** Four parts, one line each:
- **Cost:** the money, people and time asked for. Use the user's figures; if none, write `[to validate]` and say what to price.
- **What you get:** what the decision-maker will have at the end (a result, a learning, a working version).
- **When you will know:** a date or a number of weeks, and the signal that will show whether it is working.
- **Either way:** what happens next if the signal is good, and if it is not (stop, and what is lost).

Make the first ask the smallest step that would produce real evidence, usually a few weeks, not the whole programme. A small step that can be undone is easy to approve. When the real result takes months or years, time-box an early signal instead (sign-ups, a first use, a confirmed cost). If the user only said "fund it", lead with the small step and give the full cost as what follows. If they clearly want to ask for everything, write their ask and keep the smaller first step as a second option in one line; the one-pager's opening sentence then names their ask.

**d. Red-team note.** The review meeting a lone author does not have. Give the three strongest objections this decision-maker is likely to raise, written as plain statements without quotation marks (they are not the decision-maker's words), each with an honest answer (or "no good answer yet"). Then two or three **drop conditions**: specific, checkable findings that should make the author stop, such as "fewer than 3 of the first 10 people asked say they would use it". Suggest the thresholds, marked `[proposed]`, for the author to change.

**e. One-pager.** In this order: the ask in one sentence; the problem; the idea; what is known and what still needs validating; the sized ask from c; the top risk and how the first step limits it. It must fit one page (about 300 words, markers aside) and let the reader decide without reading anything else.

## 3. Hand over

Present in reading order: assumptions (if any), one-pager, press release, FAQ, red-team note, then the **to-validate list**: the `[to validate]` items with the cheapest way to check each, the ones the decision rests on first, and small ones that share a check merged into one line. Give it in the chat unless the user asks for a file. Aim for about 1,500 words in all. If it runs long, shorten FAQ answers and merge to-validate lines first; never cut the labels, the ask or the drop conditions. After a small revision show only the changed lines; after a larger one, or when asked, reissue the whole so the author has one piece to send.

End by telling the author to check the to-validate list before sending, and that the decision-maker should be told the press release is imagined if they do not know the format. If the user can reply, offer one revision round. Expect drafts: this method is meant to be rewritten, and an idea dropped on paper has done its job.

## Example

User: "I run support at a 60-person software company. I want our CEO to let me try a weekend callback service for customers who are stuck. I think it would cut cancellations but I have no data. Help me pitch it."

In outline: one question round (who would staff it, any real figures, why she believes it). She answers: two volunteers, 14 weekend tickets last month from the helpdesk report, no cancellation data. The reply then gives:

- **One-pager.** Ask: "Approve a 6-week `[proposed]` trial: two volunteers, Saturday callbacks, cost `[to validate: overtime for two people on 6 Saturdays]`." Known: 14 weekend tickets last month (helpdesk report). To validate: whether weekend waits lead to cancellations.
- **Press release**, labelled HYPOTHETICAL top and bottom, dated next spring (imagined): "Stuck on a Saturday? [Company] now calls you back the same day." No satisfaction figures, no customer quote; her own sentence, word for word, as the quote, and the reply asks her to confirm she is happy to be quoted.
- **FAQ**, including "How do you know this reduces cancellations?" answered "We do not yet. The trial compares renewals for customers who got a callback with those who waited until Monday."
- **Red-team note.** Objection: 14 tickets is too few to matter. Drop condition: fewer than 10 `[proposed]` callbacks requested over six weeks.
- **To-validate list** with the cheapest check for each item.
