# Name check: choose a name and screen it before committing

A name is cheap to change before launch and costly after. This skill gets a person to a short list quickly, then looks for reasons to drop each name while dropping is still free. Its value is the honesty of the screen, so the four rules below come before everything else.

## Four rules

1. **Say what was looked up, where and when.** Availability changes by the minute. Every statement about a domain, handle or trademark carries one of three labels:
   - **Looked up**: the source, the date and time, and what came back.
   - **Not looked up**: the exact thing for the person to check, and where.
   - **Could not look up**: what was tried and why it failed.
   Two weaker tags exist for things you did not look up at their source: "seen in web search" (a listing among search results or on someone else's page, which is not a register or registry lookup) and "from memory, not looked up". For the sound and meaning check, which is not a lookup, write "checked here, not a native-speaker check".
   Use the real clock time with its time zone; one time can cover a batch of lookups.
   Never write "available", "free" or "no conflicts" as your own conclusion, even after a lookup. Report what the source said ("no registration record found at lookup.icann.org, [date, time, zone]"), because no record does not prove the name can be had: it may be reserved, premium-priced, or taken a minute later.
2. **This is a first screen, not legal clearance.** The US trademark office itself says no search finds every conflict or gives a clear-cut answer. Say so once, plainly, in the report, and say when a professional search is worth it (step 4).
3. **Never offer, and advise against, a name that could be confused with an existing brand.** Apply this list to every hit, yours or theirs, and use the first line that fits:
   - Same or confusable name (respelling, sound-alike, near-identical) in the same or a related field, any size of brand: drop.
   - Copy, respelling or sound-alike of a famous brand, or the famous name plus a letter or suffix: drop, in any field.
   - Ordinary word that happens to sit close to a famous brand: drop in the same field, caution elsewhere.
   - Shares only an ending or fragment with a famous brand: caution, and name the brand so the person can judge.
   - Shares one ordinary word with a brand that is not famous, in the same field: caution, and name it.
   - Same or similar name as a brand that is not famous, in an unrelated field: note it and go on.

   Famous means a typical person in the audience's market would recognise it without being told the field; if unsure, treat it as famous. Dropping famous names in every field is this skill's own caution and is stricter than the US office's test. If the person brings a name that fails, tell them directly and give the reason.
4. **Do not register, buy, reserve or sign up for anything.** List the next steps for the person to take themselves.

## Which path

- **They already have a name** ("is this name taken", "can I call it X"): you need only what it is and where it will be used. Go to step 3 for that name. Offer alternatives only if the screen turns up a problem or they ask.
- **They need a name:** start at step 1.

## Step 1. Get the brief

Names need a target. You need four things:

1. What it is, in one line.
2. Who it is for and where: countries and main languages.
3. The feel they want, in two or three words, and any names they like or have ruled out.
4. Where the name must work: website, app store, social handles, said aloud, on a shop sign.

Take what the request already gives. If something that changes the answer is missing and the person is there, ask once, in one short message, at most three questions. If they are away or cannot reply, assume the most likely answer, state each assumption at the top of the report, and carry on.

## Step 2. Generate and shortlist

Give 15 to 20 candidates, 3 to 5 from each of at least four of these approaches. Different kinds of name make different trades, so show the spread:

- **Says what it does.** Clear at once, but hard to protect and hard to find in search.
- **Hints at the benefit.** Suggests without stating; the usual sweet spot.
- **Real word from elsewhere.** A familiar word with no link to the field; distinctive, needs explaining.
- **Coined or blended.** Easiest to protect and to find a domain for; means nothing until you give it meaning.
- **Two words joined.**
- **A word from the audience's own language or culture.** Only where it is theirs to use and reads well to them.
- **A person, place or story** behind the project.

Cut any name that is hard to say aloud, would be misspelled after one hearing, boxes the project into one feature, or that you recognise as an existing brand. Then shortlist 3 to 5. If the person is there, show the list by approach and let them pick or react. If they are away, pick yourself and say why in a line each.

## Step 3. Screen each shortlisted name

Keep it proportionate: about four to six lookups per name. If you can afford fewer, do not shrink the shortlist below three; thin the checks by cutting handles first, then domains, then the trademark register, and collisions last (if they asked for a domain and you have no browser for the register, spend on the domain before the register). Stop early on a name once a drop is certain. A refused lookup counts; do not retry it.

**a. Collisions (live search).** Search the name alone, the name with the field ("[name] budgeting app"), and its obvious respellings and sound-alikes. For software, also look in the app stores and package or code registries that matter. For each hit ask the two questions the US trademark office asks: is the name similar in sound, look or meaning, and are the goods or services related (same kind of thing, same buyers, used together)? Those two questions are also the test for "same or related field" in rule 3; apply its list. If a hit seen only as a search title would decide the verdict, open the page; if you cannot, say you saw the title only.

**b. Trademark registers (first look).** Use the register for the person's market: the USPTO search (tmsearch.uspto.gov) for the US, TMview for the EU, the national office elsewhere (find its search page by web search and name it), and WIPO's Global Brand Database across countries. Look for the exact name, close spellings and sound-alikes, among live marks, for related goods or services; a dead mark may still be in use, so note it. These are interactive tools: search by hand in a browser tool if you have one, and never script or bulk-query them (WIPO's terms forbid automated querying). Otherwise mark the register "not looked up" and give the person the exact searches to run. Report only what you saw ("no live identical mark seen for software in the USPTO search, [date]"); never "no trademark conflicts". Registers miss businesses that use a name without registering it, which is why step a still counts.

**c. Sound and meaning.** Say it aloud. Could someone spell it after hearing it once? Does it sound like something else? Check its meaning and slang sense in each main language of the audience, by search where you can. Say honestly that this is not a native-speaker check, and for any language other than the one you are writing in suggest asking two or three native speakers.

**d. Domains and handles.** List the exact ones worth having: the name on the main ending for their market, and one or two fallbacks that add a plain word (get-, -app, -hq) so the domain does not drive the choice of name. Look up registration records if you have a tool that can (an RDAP or WHOIS query, or ICANN's lookup at lookup.icann.org) and label each as in rule 1. A registered domain is not always a live business; say which it is if you looked. Handle pages are often blocked or misleading to read, so mark handles "not looked up" unless a page gave a clear answer.

## Step 4. Decide and report

Lead with the answer, then any assumptions you made, then the evidence:

1. **Recommended name**, with the reason in two or three lines, and a **runner-up**. A caution name can be the runner-up if you say what the caution is.
2. **One tagline** for the recommended name. One, not a list.
3. **Screen table**, one row per shortlisted name: approach; collisions found; trademark first look; sound and meaning; domains and handles; verdict. Every availability cell carries its rule 1 label. Use one of four verdicts: **clear so far** (the collision search ran and found nothing that rule 3 makes a drop or caution; mention any "note it" hit; say which checks it rests on), **caution** (say what), **drop**, or **not screened** (the collision search did not run).
4. **What was not checked**, and the line that this is a first screen, not legal clearance.
5. **When a professional search is worth it:** before filing a trademark; before spending real money on the name (packaging, signage, a paid launch, incorporation); if selling in several countries; if the field is crowded; or if the screen found anything close.
6. **Next steps for the person**, in order: recheck the domain at a registrar themselves, register the domain and handles themselves, ask native speakers, get the professional search if point 5 applies.

You may recommend a name whose trademark or domain checks are still open, as long as its collision search ran and the open checks are listed as the first next steps. If they asked for a domain and none could be looked up, give the ones to aim for in order, each with its label. If every shortlisted name fails, say so and go back to step 2 with what the screen taught you. Do not recommend the least bad name.

**When they brought their own name,** the report is shorter: the verdict and its reason, a table of each check with its label and result, points 4 to 6, and an offer of alternatives if the verdict is caution or drop (ask for the feel first if they are there; if they are away, give three to five and screen the best one). No runner-up; a tagline only if they ask.

## When something is missing

- **No web search or lookup tool:** do steps 1 and 2 (skip both if they brought a name) and the sound check from step 3c, mark every other check "not looked up", and give the person the list of searches and lookups to run. The answer at the top is that nothing was screened; the verdict is "not screened", so do not tell them to go ahead and spend. Offer alternatives if they ask or once their searches find a problem.
- **User away:** one reply with the full report; assumptions and your own shortlist as steps 1 and 2 say.
- **They want a legal opinion** ("can they sue me", "will my application pass"): give what the first screen shows and say that question is for a trademark attorney.

## Worked example

The findings here are invented to show the shape, and [Name] stands for whatever name the person brings; nothing in it was screened.

Request: "Is this name taken? I want to call my budgeting app [Name]. US launch."

They have a name, so go to step 3: search the name alone, the name with "budgeting app", and its respellings. Suppose the searches turn up an established budgeting app, [Other], whose name differs by one letter. The reply:

> **Verdict: drop.** A budgeting app called [Other] already exists, one letter away, for the same users. Similar in sound and look, same kind of service: that is the pattern the trademark office's two questions are there to catch, so I would not build on it.
>
> | Check | Label | Result |
> |---|---|---|
> | Collisions | Looked up: web search for "[Name]" and "[Name] budgeting app", [date, time, zone] | [what came back, with links] |
> | Trademark register | Not looked up | Search [NAME] and [OTHER] at tmsearch.uspto.gov, live marks, finance software |
> | Domains, handles | Not looked up | [name].com, get[name].com, @[name]; not worth your time given the verdict |
>
> Not checked: registers, domains, handles, app stores. This is a first screen, not legal clearance.
> If you still want the name, a professional search is worth it before you spend on it, because the screen found something close. Whether its owner could object is a question for a trademark attorney.
> Next: tell me the feel you are after (calm, playful, strict?) and I will suggest names from a few directions and screen the best ones the same way.
