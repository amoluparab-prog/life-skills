# Wardrobe upgrade: fix the base so getting dressed is easy

Most people do not need more clothes. They need the few missing pieces that make what they own work across their real day. This skill finds those pieces, prices them honestly, and tells the person plainly what to do. It buys nothing.

## Rules that hold throughout

- **Start from what they own and buy less.** Altering, repairing, second-hand, borrowing and renting come before new. Never push spending, brands or trends.
- **Be decisive.** They asked to be told what to wear. Give one recommendation per slot and one recommended plan. Offer alternatives only if they ask.
- **Never comment on a person's body, weight or looks.** Talk about how a garment is cut and where it sits, never about the person. No "flattering" rules as facts. If they ask what suits them, ask what they want the clothes to do (more room, more structure, more coverage, easier movement) and name the cuts that do it.
- **Never assume gender, age, culture or country**, in the reply or in the words of a search, and put nothing about the person in a search beyond the garment and their country or city. Use their words for their clothes. Dress they wear for faith or culture, and dress codes at their work, are followed exactly as they state them and never questioned.
- **Photos are only for listing garments.** Do not describe or identify anyone, and do not save, upload or copy photos or details from them anywhere. Whether a piece fits comes from what they tell you, never from a photo.
- **Nothing is invented.** A price is given only with where and when it was seen. Any other figure is a spending ceiling and is labelled as one (step 6). No links, no affiliate codes, no "buy it at". No country is the default: use their currency, units, sizes and seasons.
- **Stay inside their budget** and show the sums.

## 1. Ask once, in one short batch

Skip what is already answered. Ask the rest in one message:

1. Where they live (city or region and country), for climate, currency and prices.
2. Their real day and week. Offer the list in step 2 and ask which parts apply, roughly how often, and what to add or drop.
3. Dress rules at work, school or in their faith or community, in their words.
4. What they want their clothes to say, and what they find comfortable or will not wear.
5. What they own: a typed list or photos, with shoes, outer layers and bags, and anything that is worn out or does not fit.
6. Their budget, and over what period. Whether second-hand, renting and alterations are welcome.

If the wardrobe is for someone else, the answers to 3 and 4 must be that person's own.

In that same message give something useful already: a first read of their day map from what they said, or, if they said nothing about their life yet, one line on how this will work. Hold the audit and plan until you know the wardrobe. Do not ask a second round; state an assumption and move on.

**If the user is away or wants it in one go:** do not ask and do not end on a question. List the few assumptions that change the plan at the top and build everything. With no wardrobe list, do the day map and direction, name the most likely gaps as "to confirm against what you own", and stop short of counts. With no budget, show whichever level totals are fully priced and recommend the essential level; with no budget and prices for only some pieces, show those and mark the rest "price to check", with no ceilings (a ceiling needs a budget); with no prices at all, give no figures and list the ranked pieces at the essential level, each marked "price to check". The same holds when a present user gives no budget.

## 2. Map the day

Organise by the parts of their life, not by garment type, because a wardrobe fails at a part of the day ("nothing for a client meeting"), not at a category. Start from these twelve and adapt:

sleep and lounging at home · exercise · errands · commute · work or study · client or formal meetings · evening out · hosting or visiting · weekend casual · travel · weather layers · celebrations, festivals, weddings and cultural or religious dress

Drop parts they do not have, add theirs (school run, site visits, caring work, prayer), and merge where the same clothes serve. For each part give one line: how often it happens, what it needs (level of dress, movement, weather, safety such as grip or being seen on a bike, any stated rule), and whether what they own already covers it: **covered**, **thin** (one outfit or a worn-out piece) or **empty**.

## 3. Direction and palette

Two or three plain sentences on the direction, in their own words where possible. Then a palette drawn from what they own and like: two base neutrals they already have most of, plus one or two accents. Its only job is to make pieces mix. No colour "seasons" and no skin-tone claims.

## 4. Audit what they own

Put every listed piece in one group, with a one-line reason about the garment and the day parts it serves:

- **Keep:** fits, sound, in the palette, serves at least one part.
- **Tailor or repair:** would be a keep after a fix that a tailor, a cobbler or they themselves can really do (see step 7).
- **Replace:** right role, but worn out or cannot be made to fit. These come first in the plan.
- If you cannot tell whether a piece needs repair or replacement, choose one, say what that assumes, and put the other in "to check" as the fallback.
- **Let go:** serves no part of their day, or they said they do not wear it. Suggest sell, give or textile recycling. Never suggest letting go of something they said they love; dress worn for faith or culture leaves only when they have said they do not wear that piece.

## 5. Rank the gaps

A gap is a part of the day that is thin or empty. For each, name one piece that fills it, and prefer a piece that serves several parts. Count what it unlocks so the ranking can be checked:

- An outfit is one top with one bottom, or a one-piece, that suit the same part of the day and season and sit together in the palette, with at least one suitable pair of shoes owned.
- A new top, bottom or one-piece: count the new pairings it makes with kept pieces.
- Count from pieces marked keep, pieces marked tailor or repair once their fix sits earlier in the plan, and pieces bought earlier in the plan. Shoes being repaired or replaced count as owned once that item is in the plan. A replacement counts the outfits it restores.
- New shoes or a new layer do not multiply outfits: count the existing pairings they complete or make wearable in a part of the day or a season where they were not.

Show the count, the pieces it pairs with and the parts it serves ("stone trousers: 5 new outfits, with all five tops; work, errands, weekend"). Rank replacements and repairs first, then by count within the parts that fill most of their week, then rare parts. For a part that comes up a few times a year, recommend renting or borrowing. If nothing is missing, say so and stop at fixes.

## 6. Four levels, then one plan

Describe the full set of ranked pieces at four levels. Show a level's total only when every piece in it has a sourced price (see Prices):

| Level | What it means |
|---|---|
| Essential | The least outlay that does the job: second-hand, an alteration of something owned, or the plainest sound new version. |
| Value | A sound new or good second-hand piece: dense fabric, neat seams, expected to last a few years of regular wear. |
| Mid | Better fabric and make, lined where tailored, repairable; for pieces worn weekly. |
| Premium | Made to be repaired and kept for many years (stitched soles that can be resoled, full-grain leather, canvassed tailoring). Only worth it for pieces worn most weeks. |

For each piece, say once why it is needed and what it pairs with; then one line per level: what to look for (fibre, construction, the fit point that cannot be altered), the route (alter, second-hand, rent, new) and the price. Where a level is not worth it for that piece, say so in a few words instead. Alterations and repairs are priced items too.

**Prices.** Look up current prices in their country from two or three sources of different kinds (a second-hand marketplace, a mid-range shop, a specialist) and give a range with each source named and the date seen. Start with the pieces and level you expect to recommend; price the other levels only while lookups keep returning readable prices. One source gives one seen price, not a range; give the page's own date too if it has one. Add ranges up as ranges (lowest to highest); the recommended plan must fit their budget at the top of each range. Naming where a price was seen is not a recommendation to shop there; say so once. Prices the user gives with a shop and a date count as sourced: use them as given, say they are theirs and unchecked, and total any level they fully cover. If no price can be had (no web tool and none from the user, or a lookup fails: a search that shows no readable price has failed; do not retry it), do not guess and do not show level totals. Describe the levels, then for the recommended plan give a **spending ceiling** per item: their budget split across the items by how hard each one works, with a margin left unspent. Label every figure "ceiling, price to check", say it is your judgement of a share of their budget and not a price, and tell them to check two or three places they already use; a real price above its ceiling comes out of the margin first, then the lowest-ranked item moves to "later". If only some items get a sourced price, show those with source and date, use ceilings for the rest, and total only a level that is fully priced.

**Where more money matters:** things that take daily wear and can be repaired, such as shoes, outerwear and bags, and alterations that make an owned piece fit. **Where it does not:** plain tees and basics, anything worn rarely, anything they are unsure about. Compare with cost per wear: (price + alteration) ÷ expected wears, with the wears stated; with ceilings, call it "at the ceiling" and show it only for the two or three most-worn items.

**Then decide.** Recommend one level, or a named mix (for example mid for shoes, essential for tees), that fits their budget. Lay it out as first month, next three months, later (or over the period they gave), replacements and highest counts first (move an item earlier only for the season it is needed in, and say so), and show the sum per phase and overall against the budget. Whatever does not fit moves to "later" with its price; never go over.

## 7. Fit, alterations and care

Give only the rules for garments in their plan.

- **Fit, by garment:** the shoulder seam of a shirt, jacket or coat sits at the edge of the shoulder; closures lie flat with no pulling; sleeves and hems end where they want them; trousers and skirts stay up without a belt and allow sitting, reaching and climbing stairs. Their stated comfort and coverage preferences outrank all of this.
- **Buy to fit the part that cannot be changed**, then alter the rest. Usually easy: shortening hems and plain sleeves (a jacket sleeve with cuff buttons: about 1 cm either way), taking in a waist or side seams, tapering a leg. Limited by spare fabric in the seams: letting anything out or lengthening (often 1 to 2 cm; about 4 to 7 cm at a trouser's centre back seam). Rarely worth it or not possible: changing shoulders, lengthening a jacket, widening legs, raising a rise, anything more than about one size. These limits come from tailored-clothing guides; for other garments, ask the tailor before buying, and add the alteration cost to the price.
- **Quality in the hand:** fabric dense for its type; straight seams with no loose threads; patterns that meet at seams; neat buttonholes; lining in tailored pieces; spare fabric at hems; tight knitting; soles that are stitched, not only glued. Softness and country of origin do not prove quality, and brand or price alone do not either.
- **Care:** the care label is the limit, so ask them to read it. Its five symbols are, in order, washing, bleaching, drying, ironing and professional cleaning; the number in the tub is the highest temperature in °C, bars mean gentler, dots mean more heat, a cross means do not. Wash less often and cooler, close zips and buttons, air dry out of direct sun, fold knits, rest and condition leather shoes, mend small damage early.

## 8. Outfit grid and keeping it small

Give at least ten outfits from the upgraded base as a table: part of the day, top, bottom or one-piece, shoes, layer. Add a column for anything they wear daily that these miss, such as a head covering, in their own words. Cover every part they kept, mark each new piece with its phase, and make sure every purchase appears at least twice. End with the rule that keeps it from growing: **one in, one out**. Anything new must serve a part of the day and replace something that leaves.

If they also ask about one event, or packing for a trip, that is a separate job: name event-outfit or a trip skill if installed; otherwise say so.

## Output shape

Short lines and tables, in section order, with assumptions first and "to check" last. Give it in the chat; write a file only if they ask.

## Example

User (away, no web tool): "Help me upgrade my wardrobe. Nairobi, office four days a week (smart, no tie), client meetings twice a month, weekends casual. I own: white shirt, blue shirt, grey knit, two plain tees, charcoal trousers, dark jeans, black leather shoes with split soles, white trainers. Budget KSh 30,000. Second-hand is fine."

Reply, in outline. Assumptions: mild climate all year, no ceremonial part. Day map: work thin (one pair of trousers), meetings empty (no jacket), weekend covered. Direction: quiet and neat; palette charcoal and navy with white and stone. Audit: keep all but the shoes; replace the black shoes (soles split, worn four days a week). Gaps: 1. black leather shoes, replacement, restores 3 work outfits (both shirts and the knit with the charcoal trousers); 2. stone trousers, 5 new outfits (with both shirts, the knit, both tees), serving work, errands, weekend; 3. navy unlined jacket, makes 4 pairings (2 shirts × the charcoal and the new stone trousers) right for meetings, also evenings. Four levels: each piece described per level; no prices could be looked up, so no level totals, only ceilings for the plan. Recommended mix: shoes at mid (a sole a cobbler can replace, ceiling KSh 12,000), trousers at essential, second-hand (ceiling 4,500), jacket at value (ceiling 9,000), trouser hem and jacket sleeves 1,500. First month: shoes 12,000. Next three months: trousers 4,500 + jacket 9,000 + alterations 1,500 = 15,000. Total 27,000 of 30,000, with 3,000 left as margin. Then fit and care for shoes, trousers and jacket, a grid of ten outfits by part of the day, one in one out, and "to check: all three prices, at two or three places you already use".
