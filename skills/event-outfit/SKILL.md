# Event outfit: what to wear and what to pack

People rarely need new clothes for an event. They need to know what the dress code means for them, what the weather will do, and which of their own pieces fit both.

## Rules that hold throughout

- **Start from what they own.** Buying is the last resort, and only if they said they are willing.
- **Never comment on a person's body.** Use only the fit and comfort preferences they state. Describe clothes, not the person wearing them.
- **Respect cultural and religious dress without judgement.** What they wear for faith or culture is a fixed part of the outfit; do not ask them to explain it. Choosing among their own pieces is fine.
- **Photos are private.** Use photos of the person or their wardrobe only to list the clothes for this task. Do not identify anyone, do not describe the person, and do not save, upload or copy the photos or details from them into reminders, files or other tools.
- **Never state weather from memory.** Look it up, or say you cannot.
- **Do not assume gender** from a name or an event. Etiquette guides list "men" and "women"; give the options as routes (a suit route, a dress route, separates) and let their wardrobe decide.

## 1. Ask once, in one short batch

Skip anything already answered. Ask the rest in a single message, as a short list:

1. The event and their role (guest, speaker, host, interviewee).
2. The dress code exactly as worded on the invitation, or "none given".
3. Venue, and whether indoors, outdoors or both.
4. City and dates.
5. For several days: the schedule, day by day, and how they are travelling and where they are staying.
6. What they own that might work (a list or photos). Shoes and outer layers too.
7. Fit and comfort preferences, and anything cultural or religious to respect.
8. Whether they would buy, borrow or rent anything, and to what budget.

In that same first message give what you already can: what the dress code means (step 2), the weather if city and dates are known (step 3), and what the venue means for shoes and layers. Hold the outfits, gaps and timeline until you know the wardrobe. Do not ask a second round unless an answer changes the plan; state an assumption and move on.

**If the user is away or wants it in one go:** do not ask, and do not end on a question. Work from what they gave, list your assumptions at the top (keep to the few that change an outfit), and note beside an outfit line when it rests on one. Small things they did not list (belt, socks, underwear) are assumed owned; add them to a "check you have" line. If they listed no clothes at all, give each route as common staples and say which single piece decides it.

## 2. Decode the dress code

Use their exact wording. The usual meanings, from most to least formal:

| Wording | Usually means |
|---|---|
| White tie | Tailcoat and white bow tie, or a floor-length evening gown. Rare. |
| Black tie | Tuxedo (dinner jacket) and black bow tie, or a floor-length gown or very dressy cocktail dress. |
| Black tie optional, formal | Tuxedo or a dark suit and tie; a long gown, cocktail dress or dressy separates. |
| Creative black tie | Black tie as the base, with colour, texture or a standout accessory. |
| Cocktail, semi-formal | Dark suit, tie usual; a knee-length or midi dress, or dressy separates or jumpsuit. After 6 p.m. lean dressier. |
| Lounge suit, business formal | Matching suit and tie, or a tailored dress, or suit with skirt or trousers. |
| Festive | Cocktail level with a seasonal colour or accessory. |
| Business casual | Trousers or skirt with a collared shirt, blouse or knit; jacket optional. The employer's own norm wins. |
| Smart casual, dressy casual | Jacket or smart knit, collared shirt or dressy top, neat trousers, skirt or day dress. No tie needed. |
| Casual | Clean and neat: good jeans or chinos, plain tops, sundress. |

These meanings come from US and UK etiquette guides; the same words can mean something else in other countries and communities, so say so when the event is elsewhere.

The invitation's own wording sets the level and outranks any general guide. Then adjust within that level:

- **Time and place:** evening, city and a printed invitation push dressier; daytime, countryside and a text invite push more relaxed. When these pull apart, keep the dressier reading and let the venue change only shoes and layers.
- **Role:** an interviewee dresses one level above the workplace's normal day. A speaker needs a waistband or pocket and a collar or lapel for a microphone, and should avoid noisy jewellery and, on camera or a big screen, fine stripes or checks. A host can sit at the dressier end of their own code.
- **Weddings:** avoid white, cream or ivory as the main colour of the outfit (a dress, a suit, or a matching top and bottom), and anything bridal, unless invited to wear it. A white shirt under a suit, or one pale piece under a coloured main piece, is fine; if the only option they own is pale, say it is a judgement call and suggest checking with the couple. This never applies to a religious head covering.
- **Cultural or religious events:** customs differ between regions, communities and families, and published lists contradict each other. Give what is commonly said, label it as that, and suggest checking with the host or someone close to the family. Plan a cover-up (shawl, jacket) when a place of worship is involved. A code that names a culture ("Indian festive", "Highland dress") is not the Festive row above; treat it as ambiguous wording.

**If the wording is ambiguous, invented ("mountain chic") or missing:** say what it most likely means and why, plan for the dressier reading with a piece that can come off (tie, jacket, heels), and suggest asking the host. Offer a one-line message they can send.

## 3. Check the weather

Look up the forecast for the city and dates with a weather or web search tool. Report high, low, rain chance and wind, for the hours of the event where the source gives them, and say where and when you looked. Use the units of the event's country, with the other in brackets.

- Days the lookup does not cover (daily forecasts are not dependable past about 10 days): look up typical conditions for that place and month instead (daily figures are enough), label them as typical, and tell them to recheck 2 to 3 days before. Use the forecast for the days it does cover.
- No lookup tool: say so, ask them to paste a forecast, and meanwhile plan with a layer that covers both a warm and a cold case.
- Indoors still counts: air conditioning runs cold, marquees run hot or cold, and the walk from the car is outdoors.

## 4. Build an outfit for each occasion

For every occasion on the schedule, name pieces they listed, in this order: main pieces, shoes, layer, accessories. Add grooming once for the whole plan, not per outfit.

- **Shoes:** match the ground. Block heels, wedges or flats for grass, sand, gravel and cobbles. Only shoes already worn in; if new, say to wear them at home first.
- **Layers:** one that suits the dress code (blazer, wrap, smart coat), not a hiking jacket over a suit unless the forecast demands it.
- **Accessories and grooming:** belt, bag, tie, jewellery, watch, hosiery or socks. Grooming: pressed, lint-free, shoes cleaned; light or no scent in close quarters such as interviews.
- If they own nothing at the right level, say so plainly and give the closest outfit they can make, with what is off.

**Gaps.** List only what is really missing; if nothing is, say "none" and do not add nice-to-haves. For each: what to look for (type, colour, fabric, formality), and the cheapest route first: borrow, rent, alter something they own, then buy within their budget. No brands or shop links unless they ask.

**Backup.** Give two: a weather swap (what changes if it is colder, wetter or hotter than forecast) and a spill plan (a spare top or shirt, a darker or patterned alternative, a stain wipe in the bag).

## 5. Several days: pack to reuse

- Build around two or three colours so everything mixes. Rewear trousers, jackets and shoes; change shirts, tops and accessories.
- Give a list with counts, grouped: worn to travel, main pieces, tops, shoes, layers, accessories, underwear and sleepwear, care kit. Show which occasion each main piece serves; drop anything used only once if another piece can cover it.
- Wear the bulkiest shoes and jacket to travel. Keep the first event's outfit in hand luggage if a bag is checked.
- **Care notes:** the care label decides, so ask them to check it. Pack suits and pleats in a garment bag or folded inside-out at the shoulders. Hang everything on arrival; steam from a hot shower or a travel steamer loosens creases. Use a press cloth when ironing. Never machine wash anything labelled dry clean only or do not wash, and never steam or iron against a do-not-steam or do-not-iron symbol. For cleaning (not creases), structured jackets, silk, velvet, beading and leather go to a cleaner.

Stay with the event. For flights, lodging or packing for the rest of a trip, hand off to a trip-planning skill or say that is outside this skill.

## 6. Prep timeline and reminders

End with dated steps counted back from the first event day, or from the travel day if they travel: alterations and dry cleaning about a week before (collect two days before), a full try-on with shoes 3 to 5 days before, recheck the forecast 2 to 3 days before, pack and lay out the night before. Drop any step whose date has passed and say what that rules out (no time for alterations means choosing from what already fits).

If the session has reminder or calendar tools and the user can reply, offer to set these and create them only after a yes, using the earlier date where a step gives a range; put no photo details or body notes in them. If it has none, or the user is away, give the same steps as a plain dated checklist.

## Output shape

Scannable, with short lines: dress code in a sentence, weather with source, one block per occasion, gaps, backup, then packing list and timeline if they apply. A multi-day plan will be long; keep each block tight instead of cutting sections. State assumptions and anything you could not look up.

## Example

User: "Friend's wedding in Lisbon, Saturday 4 p.m., garden ceremony then dinner indoors. Invite says 'cocktail attire'. I own a navy suit, a grey blazer, white and light blue shirts, tan brogues, black oxfords, a burgundy tie. No budget for new stuff."

Reply, in outline (weather figures invented for this example): cocktail attire means a dark suit with a tie as the usual choice. Forecast looked up today from a named source: 24°C high, 16°C by late evening, 10% rain. Outfit: navy suit, light blue shirt, burgundy tie, tan brogues (flat soles do not sink on a lawn; the oxfords are the rain backup), suit jacket as the evening layer. Grooming: press the suit, polish the brogues. Gaps: none. Backup: white shirt packed as the spill spare; if rain is forecast on the recheck, black oxfords and an umbrella. Timeline: check the suit fits this weekend, recheck the forecast Thursday, lay everything out Friday night. Assumption stated: no part of the day is in a place of worship; if one is, keep the jacket on there.
