# week-plan

Status: tested and reviewed (two test rounds, two independent reviews, every rubric line at 4 or above). The wording changes made after the last test round have not been rerun; see the testing record. Version 0.1.0.

## What it does

A weekly review of about fifteen minutes that turns one person's commitments into a week that fits the hours they really have. You bring a pasted calendar, a task list, notes, or just answers to a few questions. If a calendar tool is connected it reads your calendar only when you ask, and only the days being planned.

It runs in three short rounds (gather; sort and count; the plan), or in one go if you are away or gave everything, and gives:

1. Carried over: each item with a verdict (do this week, defer to a date, hand on, drop).
2. Three outcomes for the week, written as finished things, across work and personal life, plus "Also this week" and "Not this week" lists so nothing is lost.
3. Hours: a row per day (window, fixed commitments, routine, free hours), then the sum: free, one fifth kept open, usable, needed, spare.
4. Protected time for the outcomes, at your best time of day where the calendar allows.
5. What to decline, delegate or defer, each with a short message draft.
6. A line of preparation for each key meeting or event.
7. One non-negotiable for health, family or rest, which is your choice.
8. A five-minute daily check.

Rules it keeps:

- The plan never holds more hours than exist, and the sum is always shown. When the work does not fit, work is cut or made smaller; it never adds hours outside the days and hours you gave.
- Guessed times get half added, and one fifth of the free hours stays open. Both are rules of thumb, not measured figures. It never makes a week fit by taking the half back off or by using the open fifth.
- Deadlines are checked one by one, not only the weekly total.
- It adds calendar blocks only after showing the exact list and getting a yes. It never moves or deletes an event and never accepts or declines an invitation.
- Messages are drafts. It sends nothing.
- No comment on how you spend your time or what you should value.
- What you share is used only for the plan: not put into web searches, not saved unless you ask.
- No medical or mental-health advice. It may suggest making an overloaded week lighter.
- No country is the default. It asks which days and hours you work and does not assume a Monday to Friday week. A public holiday is looked up only if you say where you are and a web tool is available; otherwise it goes on a "To check" list.

## How it differs from similar public skills

Several public weekly-review skills exist. Pages opened on 22 November 2026: alirezarezvani/claude-skills "weekly-review" (a three-phase Getting Things Done review with scripts that scan a workspace for open loops), charlie947/life-automation-skills "weekly-review" (works from calendar and mail connectors, read-only, three to five priorities, writes no drafts) and seabbs/skills "weekly-plan" (plans from GitHub activity, for developers). Seen in a search listing but not opened: ballred/obsidian-claude-pkm "weekly", borghei/claude-skills "weekly-review", thecraighewitt/skills "weekly-review", taylorhuston/local-life-manager "weekly-review", huytieu/COG-second-brain "weekly-checkin", kv0906 "weekly", kcwoodfield/lifeos-oss "weekly-review".

This one is narrower on purpose. It needs no connector or notes system. Its centre is the hours sum with slack, so the plan cannot exceed the free hours. It covers work and personal life in one plan, writes a draft for everything it suggests letting go, and assumes no country's working week. It is not a full inbox-and-lists clear-out. Nothing was copied from those skills.

## Example (invented)

> Help me plan my week. I work Sunday to Thursday, 8 to 4. Meetings: Sun 9 to 10 team, Mon 1 to 3 supplier review, Tue 10 to 11 one-to-one, Wed 9 to 12 workshop, Thu 2 to 3 team. Carried over: the quarterly report (third week now) and a reply to Oren about the venue. I also need the hiring shortlist done, and my son's school form is due Wednesday. I think best in the mornings. Lunch and mail take about an hour and a half a day.

In outline: it asks only for what is missing (rough times, any holiday or travel, the one thing that will not move). It proposes three outcomes: report sent, shortlist sent, school form handed in. The guesses of 8 and 4 hours become 12 and 6. The sum: 40 hours in the window, 8 fixed, 7.5 routine, 24.5 free, 4.9 kept open, 19.6 usable, 21 needed: over by 1.4. It offers ways to close the gap with the hours each saves (a shorter report, moving the shortlist, missing one meeting, which alone is not enough). The person picks the shorter report, and the plan follows: morning blocks for the report, the rest placed around the meetings, 4.9 hours shown as open, a draft to Oren, and a daily check. Nothing is added to a calendar, because nobody asked.

## Not for

A yearly calendar of renewals and deadlines (life-admin in this collection), preparing for one meeting (meet-prep), a team sprint or project plan, a trip (trip-companion), a weekend of leisure (weekend-plan), a multi-week study plan (learn-plan), a single day's plan, long-range goals, or medical or mental-health advice.

## Sources and credits

Public pages, read on 22 November 2026 through a summarising fetch tool (not verbatim). Sources 1 to 4 were fetched again by the first independent reviewer and checked against the skill.

1. David Allen Company, "Weekly Review Checklist": https://gettingthingsdone.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/10/Weekly_Review_Checklist.pdf
2. Wikipedia, "Planning fallacy": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planning_fallacy
3. Cal Newport, "Deep Habits: The Importance of Planning Every Minute of Your Work Day": https://calnewport.com/deep-habits-the-importance-of-planning-every-minute-of-your-work-day/
4. Stanford Center for Teaching and Learning, "Weekly Planning: Time Blocking Method": https://ctl.stanford.edu/weekly-planning-time-blocking-method
5. Wikipedia, "Chronotype": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronotype

What the sources support: looking back at last week's list, calendar and loose ends before planning (1); starting from fixed things, then estimating each task and placing it in a slot (4); that people underestimate their own tasks even when earlier ones ran late, and that using how long a similar thing really took helps (2); that deciding when a task will happen makes it more likely to start (2, 3); leaving time unscheduled and redrawing the plan when the day changes (3, 4); putting demanding work where the person's energy is best, which differs between people (4, 5); and a short daily re-plan (3).

Not read, so not relied on: two pages were refused by the fetch tool (Todoist's guide to the weekly review, and a 2013 Harvard Business Review article by Birkinshaw and Cohen on dropping and delegating low-value work). The sorting into decline, delegate and defer is general know-how and is not credited to them.

The author's own judgement, not taken from a source: exactly three outcomes, one of them personal; the two rules of thumb (add half to a guess, keep a fifth open). The classic thesis study in source 2 found work taking about 1.6 times the prediction, and source 4 suggests students keep 15 hours a week unscheduled; neither figure is a general rule, and the skill's figures are simpler round numbers chosen in their spirit. Also the author's: the deadline check; the order of cuts; the verdicts for carried-over items and the question about items carried three weeks or more; the 30-minute gap rule; every calendar, message, privacy and no-judgement rule; the line on distress beyond a heavy week; the wording of the daily check; and asking the person's working days in place of assuming any country's week. The skill is deliberately shorter than the full review in source 1.
