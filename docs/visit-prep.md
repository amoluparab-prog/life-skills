# visit-prep

Status: tested and reviewed (two test rounds, two independent reviews, every rubric line at 4 or above). Wording changed after the second test round and after each review has not been rerun in tests; this includes most of the current emergency wording. See the testing record. Version 0.1.0.

## What it does

Prepares a person for an appointment they attend as a patient, parent, customer or tenant: a doctor or dentist, a school or teacher, a bank or mortgage adviser, a lawyer, a government office, a landlord or a building inspection. From the kind of visit, why you are going, how long it is and what has happened so far, it writes a visit sheet of about one page:

1. The visit, with any assumptions made.
2. What you want from it: one to three things, most important first.
3. Your summary to hand over or read out in about a minute: what happened, when, what was tried. It is built only from what you say, with blanks where a fact is missing.
4. Your questions, most important first, in plain words, closing with "What happens next and when, and what would change this plan?"
5. What to bring: "The office says" (from the office's own page, with link and date read) kept apart from "Worth bringing" and "To confirm with the office".
6. Taking notes or bringing someone.
7. The last two minutes: say the plan back, ask who to contact.
8. Afterwards: what to write down, book and chase. If you come back with your notes, it turns them into a follow-up list.

Rules it keeps:

- **Preparation only.** No diagnosis, no saying what symptoms might be or what a result, letter or decision means, no treatment, legal or financial advice, and no guess at what the professional will say. Such requests become questions on the sheet.
- **Danger comes first.** If the message describes something that cannot wait for the appointment, the whole reply is a short pointer to the local emergency number or emergency care, with no question list. A danger that is not happening now gets one kind line about crisis and emergency help, then the sheet. It never tells a person that something can wait.
- **No invented requirements.** What must be brought or paid comes from the office's own page, read at the time and linked, or is marked to confirm.
- **Private details stay in the conversation.** Nothing about health, money, legal or family matters is saved or put into a web search.
- **Respect for the professional.** Neutral wording, no pressure scripts.
- **Someone else's appointment.** For a child, parent or anyone else, their own wishes go on the sheet, and they speak for themselves where they can.
- **Country-neutral.** It asks where you are and looks up the office's own rules; no country is the default.

With no web tool, or when the office's page will not open, it writes the sheet from your words and says that no official page was read.

## How it differs from similar public skills

A search on 22 November 2026 found public skills for medical visits only. DrCatHicks/informed-patient (CC BY 4.0, read through a summarising tool) researches medical evidence on a health question and ends with questions for the clinician; visit-prep does no medical research at all. Listings named "doctor-visit-prep", "medical-appointment-prep", "doctor-prep" and "psychiatry-visit-prep" were seen on skill directories, but their pages showed no content when opened, so nothing is claimed about them. No public skill was found that covers school, bank, lawyer, government office or landlord appointments. This one is a single sheet for any such appointment, with fixed limits against advice and a rule that requirements come only from the office itself. Nothing was copied from those skills.

## Example (invented)

> I have a doctor's appointment Thursday, 10 minutes, help me prepare. Knee pain for six weeks since a hike, worse on stairs, tried rest and a support. What do you think it is?

The reply, in outline: "I can't say what it is; that is the first question for the doctor." Visit: Thursday, 10 minutes, assumed in person; the office was not named, so nothing official was checked. Want: to know what is causing it, and a plan to get back to hiking. Summary: "My right knee has hurt for six weeks, since a hike on [date]. It is worse on stairs. I rested it and wore a support; [what changed]. I take [medicines]." Questions: What is causing the knee pain? What are the options for treating it? When can I expect to hike again? Closing: What happens next and when, and what would change this plan? To confirm with the office: what to bring and whether to arrive early. Worth bringing: the sheet, a list of medicines, the date of the hike. Last two minutes: say the plan back and ask who to call if it gets worse. Afterwards: write it down that day and book anything agreed.

## Not for

Medical, legal or money advice itself; a work, client or networking meeting (meet-prep in this collection); a job interview; reading a contract before signing (agreement-check); comparing quotes (quote-check); a calendar of renewals and deadlines (life-admin). A court hearing or a police interview was not designed for or tested.

The author's own judgement, not taken from a source: every limit (no diagnosis, no interpreting, no advice, no predicting the professional); the whole danger rule and its wording; the privacy rule; the split between "The office says", "Worth bringing" and "To confirm with the office"; the number of questions by slot length, the summary and page sizes; the standing question "what would change this plan"; the prompts for government office, landlord and inspection visits; asking before recording; putting an adult relative's wishes first; and neutral wording about every professional. The sources come from four countries, but no page on any country's office rules is a source of the skill; those are looked up at use time.
