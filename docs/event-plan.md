# event-plan

Status: tested and reviewed (two test rounds, two independent reviews, every rubric line at 4 or above). The wording changes made after the second test round and after each review have not been rerun in tests, and no test read a live agenda page. Version 0.1.0.

## What it does

Helps one person get the most from an event they are attending: a conference, summit, trade show, offsite or large networking event, in person or online. From the event (a link or a pasted agenda), what you want from it, your limits and who you already know there, it writes one plan:

1. Two or three goals you can check on the last day.
2. Sessions marked "must" or "if free", each tied to a goal, with clashes settled and at least one slot left open each half-day on purpose. For a trade show, stands by hall in walking order with what to ask at each.
3. People worth meeting, taken from the public speaker, exhibitor and organiser lists and from who you know, each with a reason, where to find them and an opening question.
4. Up to five short meeting requests, each to one named person, for you to edit and send.
5. A one-line introduction and a question to ask in return.
6. A plan for each day with walking time, meals as fixed appointments, breaks, and ten minutes for notes.
7. A note template: one note per conversation (who, where, what, promised, next step and date).
8. The week after: what to send by when, with drafts that have blanks for what will only be known afterwards.

Rules it keeps:

- Nothing invented. Every session, speaker, room, stand and agenda time comes from the agenda you pasted or a page it opened. Times it adds itself (walks, breaks, a meal the agenda does not time) are marked "suggested".
- The agenda is dated ("as read on") and you are told to check it again the day before.
- About people: only what the event's pages and their public professional work say. Nothing private; no gender or title guessed from a name.
- Speakers and exhibitors only. It does not collect or copy attendee lists and does not guess email addresses.
- Drafts only, one person at a time. It sends nothing, and never writes a message meant for many people. One reminder at most; a no ends it.
- One reminder to read the event's code of conduct, and one that your employer's travel, expense, gift and confidentiality rules are yours to follow.
- Country-neutral: times in the venue's time zone; for an event abroad, local business customs are looked up on a named page or left on a To do list, never guessed from a name or nationality.

If the agenda cannot be read (no web tool, a page that will not open, not published yet), it says so, gives the parts that need no agenda in full and a short form of the rest with blanks, and ends with a To do list.

## How it differs from similar public skills

Several public skills cover parts of this job. Opened during the build: amplemarket "conference-networking-prep" (researches speakers, sponsors and attendees against a sales customer profile), lyndonkl "conf-schedule-optimization" (a session timetable only) and thecraighewitt "event-networking" (event prospecting for sales teams). Seen in a search listing but not opened: claudskills.com "conference-prep", kilo.ai "conference-game-plan", clawdbot "conference-schedule-optimizer", lyndonkl "conf-program-extraction", msg2ai "conference-team-skills", codaone "phy-event-gtm", amplemarket "conference-event-lead-finder". This one is narrower on purpose: it is for any single attendee and not a sales team, it covers the whole event in one plan, it needs no connectors, and its own ground is the fixed limits above. Nothing was copied from those skills.

## Example (invented names)

> I'm going to the Larchfield Packaging Summit next week, help me plan it. I sell labelling machines for a small firm and want customers. Agenda pasted below.

The reply, in outline: the agenda dated as read from the pasted text, with the two reminders. Goals: six conversations with people who run packing lines, two demo visits booked, learn what buyers ask about changeover. Day 1: the 11:30 talk "Cutting changeover time" is a must; the talk it clashes with serves no goal and is skipped; the 14:00 buyer round tables are a must and need booking now; the keynote slot is left open. People: the changeover speaker, Odalys Brenninkmeyer of Tindle Foods, with the role left to check because the agenda does not give it, and the question "What was the hardest part of the changeover work to get accepted?" A meeting request draft that opens "Hello Odalys Brenninkmeyer" and asks for ten minutes after the talk. Introduction: "I sell labelling machines for a small firm, and I'm here to meet people who run packing lines." A day line with suggested times marked as suggested, the note template, and a follow-up draft with blanks.

## Not for

Organising or hosting an event (host-plan in this collection covers gatherings at home), being a guest at a wedding or party, a brief for one meeting (meet-prep), travel and packing (trip-companion), what to wear (event-outfit), or a job-search campaign (launchpad).

What the sources support: starting from goals (1, 2, 3, 4); ranking sessions because you cannot attend everything, booking the ones that need it, and useful conversations outside the rooms (1, 2, 3); choosing a few people from the programme, reading their work and arranging meetings ahead (1, 3, 4, 6); a short introduction (1, 3, 4); breaks, lunch as an appointment, time between meetings, the floor map and a route (1, 6, 7); a note per contact (1, 5); a quick personal follow-up, a short call where there is a reason, a check-in months later (1, 4, 5, 8); the code of conduct, social events as professional settings and power imbalances (1); rechecking the agenda and stand plan (2, 6).

Where the skill departs from them on purpose: source 4 suggests reviewing attendee lists where available; the skill does not, and leaves the event's own opt-in meeting tool to the user. Source 2 suggests filling free slots with meetings; the skill keeps open slots, which may hold booked meetings but are not filled with sessions. Source 3 says photographing slides for private use is usually fine and asks you to respect signs about sharing online; the skill's "respect signs that forbid photographing" goes a little further.

The author's own judgement, not taken from a source: every number (three goals at most; at most four questions; three musts a day; one open slot each half-day; up to six must stands; five to eight people for two or three days and three or four for one; three to five requests under about 80 words; one or two lookups per person and ten in all; sending one to two weeks ahead; one reminder; about twenty words for the introduction; a 10 to 15 minute walking allowance; ten minutes for notes; top contacts within two working days, the rest within a week, a check-in in about three months; the reply sizes). The sources say only "as soon as you can" for follow-up and "a few months" for a check-in. Also the author's: the limits on researching people, the source labels, marking added times "suggested", leaving a session that serves no goal, saying what to do about the session not chosen in a clash, and the short plan when the agenda cannot be read.
